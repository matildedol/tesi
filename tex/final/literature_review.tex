This section presents the state of the art and how the present work relates to it.

The behaviour of the test error of tree-based models as a function of their complexity has itself been the object of debate. \cite{belkin2019reconciling} report a double descent curve for tree-based models, where the test error decreases again beyond the interpolation threshold. \cite{curth2023uturn} show that this second descent arises from combining distinct complexity axes into a single one, such as the number of leaves of a tree and the number of trees in an ensemble, and that the curves recover a traditional convex shape when plotted against an effective number of parameters. In our setting, complexity is measured along a single axis, the maximum depth $d$ of a single decision tree. Along this axis, once the learned tree interpolates the training sample, a larger depth no longer changes the learned model, and the empirical test error saturates (Section~\ref{sec: unionbound}); a second descent can only arise by moving to a different complexity axis, such as the number of trees in an ensemble.

Several works evaluate generalization bounds for decision trees in practice, but with a motivation different from ours. Specifically,~\cite{kaariainen2004selective} use Rademacher complexity as an empirical complexity measure: they compute the Rademacher penalty $\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\big|\frac1n\sum_i\xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\big|$ on a single realization of $D$ and $\xi$, and plug it into a high-probability bound on $R(h)$. Their hypotheses set is data-dependent, namely the prunings of a tree grown on a separate sample, for which reduced error pruning applied to relabeled data computes the supremum exactly. They compare this bound with an Occam's razor bound based on a uniform code length over the prunings, analogous to our Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}. Our work differs in that we consider hypotheses sets $\cH_d$ fixed in advance and aim at interpretable analytical expressions rather than numerical values; hence, we use $\mathcal R_n(\LL)$ only as an intermediate step towards $N_D(\cH)$.

In~\cite{leboeuf2022generalization}, the authors derive an analytical upper bound on the growth function of classes of binary decision trees with fixed structure, that is, a structure-dependent count of the distinct classifications that such trees can realize on a sample, more detailed than the counting inequality $\Pi_n(\cH)\le\min\{\vert\cH\vert,2^n\}$ of Section~\ref{sec: growthf}. They use it within a structural risk minimization bound as a pruning criterion, evaluated through the test accuracy of the pruned trees. Our interest is different: rather than using a bound for model selection, we compare its shape with the empirical generalization gap as a function of the complexity of the hypotheses set.

Other works compare the numerical tightness of several generalization bounds on benchmark datasets~\cite{kaariainen2005comparison,pichuka2007tighter}, or use bounds on the VC dimension of decision trees for pruning~\cite{yildiz2015vc}. Further papers propose bounds on the error of decision trees that depend on the specific training sample or on an effective number of leaves~\cite{mansour2000generalization,golea1998generalization,bartlett2002rademacher}, without evaluating them empirically. Outside decision trees,~\cite{jiang2019fantastic} test a large number of complexity measures against the generalization of neural networks.

Although not specific to decision trees,~\cite{boucheron2005theory} is a survey on statistical bounds on the risk of learning algorithms, and has been a fundamental guide for the theoretical side of this thesis. A difference, however, lies in the emphasis of the analysis. The survey is largely concerned with how bounds behave as the sample size $n$ grows, as guarantees on the precision of the empirical risk in estimating the true risk. The same perspective underlies the statistical analyses of CART~\cite{klusowski2024large}. We, instead, keep $n$ fixed and regard bounds as functions of the complexity of the hypotheses set, which should describe the qualitative behaviour of the generalization gap. For instance, our Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}, analogous to Theorem 5.1 of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, is crucial to this work because it allows us to include the noise in the bounds, whereas in~\cite{boucheron2005theory} it is mainly used to improve the dependence on $n$ when the training error goes to $0$.

All in all, there is a wide literature on theoretical bounds on the risk, improving on their interpretability or tightness, which has been a fundamental compass for the mathematical roadmap of this thesis. On the empirical side, several works evaluate such bounds in practice, but with the aim of selecting models or certifying their accuracy, rather than describing and interpreting the generalization behaviour of decision trees. Our work positions itself as a story of theory and evidence aimed at explaining the generalization of decision trees rigorously. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first work that compares the shape of theoretical bounds, as functions of the complexity of the hypotheses set, with the empirical generalization gap of decision trees.
