\begin{abstract}
This thesis investigates the descriptive possibilities and limits of classical generalization bounds. We study this by comparing theoretical results and empirical evidence with experiments on binary decision trees under the zero-one loss. Our aim is to obtain a bound that qualitatively captures the behavior of the generalization gap as model complexity (maximum depth) increases and as aspects of the learning problem vary. The final result is a bound that accounts for the finite nature of our problem by counting hypotheses after observing the data. Moreover, it is sensitive to different noise levels in the data as it depends on the best risk attainable over the hypothesis class. 
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Setting and Approach}
Given a sample space $\Omega$, let inputs and labels be generated by $X:\Omega \to \mathcal X = \{\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_N\}$ and $Y: \Omega \to \mathcal Y = \{-1, 1\}$. Let $p$ be the joint probability distribution of $(X,Y)$ on $\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y$, with
\[
 p_{j,y}=\Pr[X=\bar x_j,Y=y],\qquad
 p_{j,y}\ge0,\qquad
 \sum_{j=1}^N\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}p_{j,y}=1.
\]
Let $D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n$ be a dataset of $n$ i.i.d.\ copies of $(X,Y)$ drawn from $p$. We use the term {\it dataset\/} to refer to the {\it random variable\/} generating the data throughout this work. We denote by $S = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n$ a realization of it. Given this data, we want to learn a classifier $h:\X\to\Y$ that given a new input $x\in\X$ classifies it correctly. 
Let $\hyp \subseteq \{h:\mathcal X \to \Y\}$ be a nonempty, finite set of functions where we look for such a classifier.
Given a loss function $\ell:\Y \times \mathcal Y \to \mathbb R^+$, let
\[
    R(h) \coloneq \mathbb E(\ell(h(X), Y)) = \sum_{\bar x\in\mathcal X} \sum_{\bar y \in\{-1,1\}} \ell(h(\bar x), \bar y) p(\bar x, \bar y).
\]
be the true risk (or test error), and
\[
    \hat R_D (h) \coloneq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(X_i), Y_i).
\]
the empirical risk (or training error), for a fixed $n$. 
Because we do not have access to $p$, we select the {\it empirical risk minimizer\/} (ERM), 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: ERM}
    \hat h_D \in \argmin_{h\in \hyp} \hat R_D (h)
\end{equation}
which, importantly, is a random variable of the data $D$. Note that at least one such $\hat h_D$ exists, since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty. 

We study bounds on the expected difference between the test and train error of the ERM,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: gen gap}
\expv\left[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right]
\end{equation}
that is called {\it generalization gap}. We state our first results in a general setting, for arbitrary hypothesis classes and loss functions. As our study develops, we use results that are specific to the zero-one loss, i.e.\
\begin{equation}\label{eq: counting loss}
    \ell(h(x),y) = \mathbf{1} (h(x)\neq y) = \frac{1 - h(x)y}{2},
\end{equation}
that is also used for the experiments. 
On the experimental side, in fact, we consider the algorithm of binary decision trees with decision rules at the nodes of type $x\ge t$ for $t\in\R$, trained with the zero-one loss. So, for a fixed depth $d$, our hypothesis class $\hyp_d$ is the class of functions representable by binary decision trees of depth at most $d$. This is the class of piecewise constant functions $h:\X \to \{1,-1\}$ with at most $2^d$ contiguous blocks, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq: def trees}
\hyp_d \coloneq \left\{ h:\X \to \{1,-1\} : \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} \mathbf{1}_{h(\bar x_j) \neq h(\bar x_{j+1})} \le 2^{d}-1 \right\} \qquad \text{for } d>0.
\end{equation} 
It is useful to define the following quantities. For a fixed $n$ and variable $d$, let
\[
\mathcal T(d) \coloneq \expv[R(\hat h_{D,d})], \qquad \mathcal L(d) \coloneq \expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_{D,d})], \qquad \mathcal G(d)\coloneq \mathcal T(d)-\mathcal L(d)
\]
be the true risk, the (expected) empirical risk, and the generalization gap of decision trees with depth at most $d$ respectively. In this notation, we look for functions $\mathcal B(d)$ such that 
\[
\mathcal G(d) \lesssim \mathcal B(d),
\]
from which 
\[
\mathcal T(d) \lesssim \mathcal B(d) + \mathcal L(d).
\]
A bound on the gap is a bound on the true risk when $\mathcal L(d)$ is $0$ or negligible. For visualization purposes, we obtain the curves $\mathcal B(d)$ by heuristically rescaling the bounds stated in the theorems to make them comparable in magnitude to the empirical curves. This reflects our focus on the qualitative dependence of the bounds on $d$, rather than on their numerical values.

The following sections are organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec: prel} provides some preliminary results used throughout. In Sections~\ref{sec: unionbound} to~\ref{sec: bestrisk}, we present theoretical results, describe the experimental design and plot empirical curves and qualitative bounds, and then comment on the results. Finally, in Section~\ref{sec: concl} we conclude and discuss limitations and potential developments of our analysis. 
Appendix~\ref{app: prel exp} and~\ref{app: code} contain some preliminary experiments and additional details on the experimental design, respectively.

\section{Preliminary Results}\label{sec: prel}

In this section, we state some preliminary results that are central to the results developed in the following sections. 

The following is a fundamental result on the moment generating function of a random variable. 

\begin{theorem}[Hoeffding's lemma]\label{app: hoeffdinglemma} [\cite{boucheron2013concentration}, Lemma 2.2]
  Let $Z$ be a real-valued random variable with $\expv[Z]=0$ and $\vert Z\vert\le a$ almost surely for some $a\in\R$. Then, for every $t\in\R$,
  \[
  \expv\left[e^{tZ}\right] \le e^{t^2a^2/2}.
  \]
\end{theorem}
The reference states it for $Z\in[a',b']$ with bound $e^{t^2(b'-a')^2/8}$; here $[a',b']=[-a,a]$. See also \cite{hoeffding1963probability}.

The following theorems are established concentration inequalities, i.e.\ upper bounds on the deviation of a random variable from its expected value. While Hoeffding's mainly uses the bounded interval over which the random variable ranges, Bernstein's and Chebyshev's are more refined results using its second moment. 

\begin{theorem}[Hoeffding's inequality]\label{app: hoeffding} [\cite{hoeffding1963probability}, Theorem 2], [\cite{boucheron2013concentration}, Theorem 2.8]
  Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be independent real-valued random variables with $Z_i\in[a_i,b_i]$ almost surely for some $a_i,b_i\in\R$, and let $\mu = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \expv[Z_i]$. Then, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i - \mu \ge \varepsilon\right] \le \exp\left(-\frac{2n^2\varepsilon^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i-a_i)^2}\right).
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Chebyshev's inequality]\label{app: chebyshev} [\cite{boucheron2013concentration}, Section 2.1]
  Let $Z$ be a real-valued random variable with finite variance. Then, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\vert Z - \expv[Z]\vert \ge \varepsilon\right] \le \frac{\Var(Z)}{\varepsilon^2}.
  \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Bernstein's inequality]\label{app: bernstein} [\cite{boucheron2013concentration}, Theorem 2.10]
  Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be independent real-valued random variables with $\expv[Z_i]=0$ and $Z_i\le M$ almost surely for some $M>0$, and let $v = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(Z_i)$. Then, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i \ge \varepsilon\right] \le \exp\left(-\frac{n\varepsilon^2}{2(v + M\varepsilon/3)}\right).
  \]
\end{theorem}
The reference bounds $\Pr[\sum_i Z_i\ge t]$ in terms of $\sum_i\expv[Z_i^2]$, which equals $nv$ since $\expv[Z_i]=0$; the statement above follows by taking $t=n\varepsilon$.

The following proposition is the main tool used in Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} that provides the first generalization gap considered in this study. 

\begin{proposition}[Union bound]\label{app: unionbound} [\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Appendix C]
  For any finite or countable collection of events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\bigcup_i A_i\right] \le \sum_i \Pr[A_i].
  \]
\end{proposition}

Tail integration is a crucial tool which allows us to go from results on the probability that a random variable is large, like the ones above, to results on its {\it expected\/} value. 

\begin{proposition}[Tail integration]\label{app: tail int} [\cite{vershynin2018high}, Lemma 1.2.1]
  For any non-negative random variable $Z$,
  \[
  \expv[Z] = \int_0^\infty \Pr[Z\ge \varepsilon] \de \varepsilon.
  \]
\end{proposition}
The reference uses $\Pr[Z>\varepsilon]$; the two integrands differ on at most countably many $\varepsilon$, hence the integrals coincide.

Finally, the Gaussian tail bound is used in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}. 

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian tail bound]\label{app: gtailbound} [\cite{vershynin2018high}, Proposition 2.1.2]
  For every $c>0$ and $x_0>0$,
  \[
  \int_{x_0}^\infty e^{-cx^2}\de x \le \frac{1}{2cx_0}e^{-cx_0^2}.
  \]
\end{proposition}
The reference states $\int_{t}^\infty e^{-u^2/2}\de u\le \frac1t e^{-t^2/2}$ for $t>0$; the statement above follows by the change of variables $u=\sqrt{2c}\,x$, $t=\sqrt{2c}\,x_0$.

{\bf Assumptions on the loss function}

Consider the following assumption on the loss function $\ell$. 

\begin{assumption}[Boundedness of loss function]\label{ass: bounded loss}
    Given a set $A\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\times A\to\R_+$,
    $\exists M\in\R, M >0$ s.t.
    \[
    \ell (a,a') \le M \qquad \forall a,a'\in A 
    \]
\end{assumption}

A bounded loss function is crucial for obtaining all the upper bounds on the gap discussed in this work. 

\begin{fact}
    The zero-one loss satisfies Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss} with $M=1$.
\end{fact}

\begin{fact}
  If the label space $\Y$ is finite, then any loss function $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R_+$ is bounded.  
\end{fact}

The previous facts show that Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss} is always satisfied in our setting. 

Nonetheless, we now give a sufficient condition that allows us to circumvent boundedness. This is useful for generalizing the results obtained in this work to other learning algorithms with real-valued predictors.

\begin{assumption}[Lipschitzness of loss function and bound at zero]\label{ass: lipsch loss}
Given two sets $A,B\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\times B\to\R_+$, 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\exists C>0$ s.t.
        \[ \vert \ell(a,b)-\ell(a',b')\vert \le C\left(\vert a-a'\vert + \vert b-b'\vert\right) \qquad \forall a,a'\in A,\ b,b' \in B\]

        \item $\exists C^\prime >0$ s.t.
        \[ \vert \ell(0,0)\vert \le C^\prime
        \]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop: suff assump}
    Let $A,B\subseteq[-K,K]$ for some constant $K>0$, with $0\in A$, $0\in B$. Then Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss} $\Rightarrow$ Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss}. 

    \begin{proof}
    Let $a\in A$ and $b\in B$. By the triangle inequality and the two points of Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, applied with $(a',b')=(0,0)$,
    \[
    \vert\ell(a,b)\vert \le \vert\ell(0,0)\vert + \vert\ell(a,b)-\ell(0,0)\vert \le C^\prime + C\left(\vert a\vert+\vert b\vert\right) \le C^\prime + 2CK.
    \]
    Since $\ell\ge0$, Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss} holds with $M = C^\prime+2CK>0$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

Therefore, if the inputs are bounded, Lipschitzness is sufficient to obtain boundedness.

On top of this,
let $T:\R\to[-1,1]$,
\begin{equation}
    T(a) = \begin{cases}
        -1 & a \le -1\\
        a & -1<a<1, \qquad\qquad a\in A\\
        1 & a\ge 1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

be a {\it truncation map\/}. 
For any hypothesis class $\hyp = \{h:\X\to\Y\}$, we can always define $\hyp_T=\{ h_T\coloneq T\circ h : \X\to[-1,1] \vert h\in\mathcal{H}\}$ and take $A = \{(h(x),y) : h\in\hyp_T, x\in\X, y\in\Y = \{1,-1\}\}$ which satisfies the assumption of bounded inputs required by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. 

Therefore, Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump} and the truncation technique determine that Lipschitzness of the loss function immediately implies boundedness. The former is easier to verify, as Example~\ref{ex: logistic} shows, and does not depend on the hypotheses set.  

\begin{example}[Logistic loss function]\label{ex: logistic}
    Let $\ell (a, b) = \log (1+e^{-ab})$, $a\in A$, $b\in B$, with $A,B\subseteq[-K,K]$ for some $K>0$, be the logistic loss function.
    Its partial derivatives are
    \[
    \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a}(a,b) = -\frac{b}{1+e^{ab}}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b}(a,b) = -\frac{a}{1+e^{ab}},
    \]
    hence, since $\frac{1}{1+e^{ab}}<1$,
    \[
    \left\vert \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial a}(a,b)\right\vert \le \vert b\vert \le K, \qquad \left\vert \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b}(a,b)\right\vert \le \vert a\vert \le K \qquad \forall a\in A,\ b\in B.
    \]
    By the mean value theorem applied to each argument separately,
    \[
    \vert \ell(a,b)-\ell(a',b')\vert \le \vert \ell(a,b)-\ell(a',b)\vert + \vert \ell(a',b)-\ell(a',b')\vert \le K\left(\vert a-a'\vert + \vert b-b'\vert\right),
    \]
    so $C = K$. With the truncation map and $\Y=\{1,-1\}$ we have $K=1$, hence $C=1$.
    Also,
    \[
    \ell(0,0) = \log 2 = C^\prime.
    \]
\end{example}

\section{A First Approach}\label{sec: unionbound}

In this section, we present our first bound on the generalization gap and we compare it with the empirical curve in the setting of binary decision trees.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm: unionbound} Let $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R_+$ be a loss function bounded by $M\in\R_+$. Then
\[                
  0 \le \expv[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le 2M\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp \vert}{2n}}
\]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Adapted from~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 2.13: the original is a high-probability bound for a finite class; here we state it in expectation, using tail integration, and we add the lower bound, which is specific to the ERM.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}]
We prove the lower bound first. Since $\hat h_D \in \argmin_{h\in\hyp} \hat R_D(h)$, we have
\[
\hat R_D(\hat h_D) \le \hat R_D(h) \qquad \forall h\in\hyp,
\]
and taking expectations
\[
\expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)] \le \expv[\hat R_D(h)] \qquad \forall h\in\hyp.
\]
For a fixed $h\in\hyp$, which does not depend on $D$, the empirical risk is an unbiased estimate of the true risk, by linearity and because the $(X_i,Y_i)$ are distributed as $(X,Y)$,
\[
\expv[\hat R_D(h)] = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \expv\left[\ell(h(X_i),Y_i)\right] = R(h).
\]
Hence
\[
\expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)] \le R(h) \qquad \forall h\in\hyp.
\]
Now take $S$ a realization of $D$ and consider in particular $h=\hat h_{S}\in\hyp$,
\[
\expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)] \le R(\hat h_{S}).
\]
Since this holds for every realization $S$ of $D$, we have $\expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)] \le R(\hat h_D)$ almost surely and, taking expectations,
\[
\expv[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)] \le \expv[R(\hat h_D)].
\]

Next, we prove the upper bound. Fix $h\in\hyp$ and let $Z_i \coloneq -\ell(h(X_i),Y_i)$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, so that
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i - \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \expv[Z_i] = (R - \hat R_D)(h).
\]
We want to apply Hoeffding's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffding}). The $Z_i$ are independent, since the $(X_i,Y_i)$ are, and $Z_i\in[-M,0]$ by non-negativity of the loss function and Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. Hence, with $a_i = -M$ and $b_i = 0$, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{app/eq: hoeffding fixed h}
\Pr\left[(R - \hat R_D)(h) \ge \varepsilon\right] \le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}.
\end{equation}
Now, the ERM $\hat{h}_D$ is a random variable dependent on the data $D$. To bound its deviation, we require the concentration event to hold simultaneously for all functions $h \in \hyp$. Therefore, by the union bound (Proposition~\ref{app: unionbound}) and~(\ref{app/eq: hoeffding fixed h}),
\begin{align}\label{app/eq: unionbound prob}
\Pr\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \ge \varepsilon\right] &\le \Pr\left[\bigcup_{h\in\hyp}\left\{(R - \hat R_D)(h)\ge\varepsilon\right\}\right] \notag \\
&\le \sum_{h\in\hyp} \Pr\left[(R - \hat R_D)(h)\ge \varepsilon\right] \notag \\
&\le \card e^{-2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}.
\end{align}

To obtain a bound in expected value from~(\ref{app/eq: unionbound prob}), we tail integrate. Let $X\coloneq(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)$ and $X^+\coloneq\max\{X,0\}$, which is non-negative, so that by Proposition~\ref{app: tail int}
\[
\expv[X] \le \expv[X^+] = \int_0^\infty \Pr[X^+\ge\varepsilon]\de\varepsilon = \int_0^\infty \Pr[X\ge\varepsilon]\de\varepsilon.
\]
Let $\alpha\coloneq\card$ and $c\coloneq 2n/M^2$, so that~(\ref{app/eq: unionbound prob}) reads $\Pr[X\ge\varepsilon]\le\alpha e^{-c\varepsilon^2}$. This bound is good for large values of $\varepsilon$, but for small $\varepsilon$ it exceeds $1$, hence we use instead
\[
\Pr[X\ge\varepsilon] \le \min\{1,\alpha e^{-c\varepsilon^2}\}.
\]
We split the integral at the threshold $\varepsilon_0$ where $\alpha e^{-c\varepsilon_0^2} = 1$, i.e.\ $\varepsilon_0 = \sqrt{\log\alpha/c}$. Using the Gaussian tail bound (Proposition~\ref{app: gtailbound}) for the second integral, we obtain
\[
\expv[X] \le \int_0^{\varepsilon_0} 1\de\varepsilon + \int_{\varepsilon_0}^\infty \alpha e^{-c\varepsilon^2}\de\varepsilon
\le \varepsilon_0 + \frac{\alpha}{2c\varepsilon_0}e^{-c\varepsilon_0^2}
= \sqrt{\frac{\log\alpha}{c}} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{c\log\alpha}},
\]
where in the last equality we used $\alpha e^{-c\varepsilon_0^2}=1$. Finally, assuming $\alpha\ge e^{1/2}$, which holds as soon as $\card\ge 2$, we have $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c\log\alpha}}\le\sqrt{\frac{\log\alpha}{c}}$, and hence
\[
\expv[X] \le 2\sqrt{\frac{\log\alpha}{c}} = 2M\sqrt{\frac{\log\card}{2n}}.
\]
It remains to consider the case $\card=1$, where the bound reads $0$ and the argument above does not apply ($\log\alpha=0$ makes $\varepsilon_0=0$ and the last step fails). If $\hyp=\{h\}$, then $\hat h_D=h$ for every $D$, which does not depend on the data, so by the unbiasedness of the empirical risk for a fixed hypothesis shown above
\[
\expv[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] = R(h) - \expv[\hat R_D(h)] = 0,
\]
and both inequalities hold with equality.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

This result formally demonstrates that the empirical risk of the ERM systematically underestimates its true risk, with the generalization gap scaling as $\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log \card}{n}}\right)$.
The logarithmic dependence on $\card$ arises by first establishing the exponential concentration of $\hat{R}_D(h)$ for a fixed hypothesis $h \in \hyp$ with Hoeffding's inequality, then taking a union bound over the class of functions $\hyp$, and tail integrating. 

Note that while the upper bound is valid for any hypothesis $h\in\hyp$, the lower bound is specific to the ERM. We next present the experimental design to evaluate the upper bound for binary decision trees and compare it with the empirical test error curve on a qualitative level. 

{\bf Experimental design}

\paragraph{Data-generating process} We create the support $\mathcal X$ as a discretization over $N= 10^9$ evenly spaced numbers of the open interval $(0,10)$. We set $N$ large enough so that it resembles the infinite-dimensional case. Then, we sample from $\X$ with the uniform measure $p_\X(\bar x) = \sum_{\bar y\in \Y}p(\bar x, \bar y) = \frac{1}{N}$. 
The law to generate the labels is
\[
y \leftarrow \sgn(f^*(x) + \sigma\varepsilon)
\] 
where $\sgn(x) = 1$ if $x\ge 0$ and $-1$ otherwise. $f^*:\X\to\R$ is the data-generating function, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is a Gaussian noise, and $\sigma>0$ controls the noise level. 
First, we set $f^*$ to a centered parabola, so that roughly half of the labels are $1$ and half $-1$ in the interval $(0,10)$. 
Since an algorithm could learn such a function with few datapoints (see preliminary experiments in Appendix~\ref{app/ sec: datagen}), we switch to a sinusoid with varying frequency. Namely, we use
\begin{equation}\label{eq: sine}
    f^*(x) = 5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right)
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the number of times $f^*$ crosses the $x$-axis in the open interval $(0,10)$, yielding $m+1$ contiguous blocks of $1$s and $-1$s. 
We set as default value $m=10$.

We test a Bernoullian noise instead of a Gaussian one, concluding that there is no interesting difference in the performance of the algorithm as long as the portion of misclassified inputs due to the noise is the same (see preliminary experiments in Appendix~\ref{app/ sec: noise}). Hence, we fix the noise level to $20\%$ and find that for $m = 10$, $\sigma \approx 3.5$ achieves it on average. 

All in all, our labels are generated as
 \[
    y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}\left(5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right) + \sigma\varepsilon\right)
 \]
 with $\sigma \approx 3.5$ and $m = 10$. All the hyperparameters of the experiments are summarized in Table~\ref{app/tab: hyperparameters} in Appendix~\ref{app: hyper}.

\paragraph{Experiment} We generate a test set with size $n_\text{test} = 200,000$ sampling from $\X$ with $p_\X$ and with replacement, and generating the labels as above. We select a range of depths that allows us to observe the asymptotic behavior of the empirical curves, namely up to $30$. Then, for each depth $d = 1,\ldots, 30$, for $T=100$ iterations, we 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(1)] generate a train set ($n= 500$);
    \item[(2)] fit a decision tree with depth at most $d$ (DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm from the scikit-learn library);
    \item[(3)] compute the train error, the test error, and the difference between the two (the gap).
\end{enumerate}
Then, we average the resulting quantities over the $T$ samples, obtaining empirical estimates of the expected values of the train error, test error, and gap over the disorder of the data. 

\paragraph{Learning algorithm} To fit decision trees, we use the DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm from the scikit-learn library. It uses the same decision rule at the nodes that we assumed in the theoretical part of this thesis, namely $x\ge t$ for arbitrary $t\in (0,10)$. However, it is a greedy algorithm, which does not always select the ERM~(\ref{eq: ERM}). See Section~\ref{sec: concl} for a discussion of this difference. 

\paragraph{Cardinality of the hypothesis class} To plot the theoretical bound, instead, we need to express the cardinality $\vert \hyp_d\vert$ in terms of the maximum depth $d$. 
\begin{fact}[Upper bound on $\vert \hyp_d\vert$]\label{fact: upp b card}
Since $\hyp_d \subseteq \{h:\X \to \Y\}$, $\vert \X\vert = N$ and $\vert \Y \vert = 2$, we have 
\[
\vert \hyp_d\vert \le 2^N \qquad \forall d>0.
\]
\end{fact}
Recall the definition of the hypothesis class~(\ref{eq: def trees}). A function with exactly $r$ blocks is determined by the choice of the label in the first block ($2$ options) and by the position of the remaining $r-1$ thresholds, which are chosen among the $N-1$ intervals $(\bar x_j, \bar x_{j+1})$ for $j =1,\ldots, N-1$: there are $\binom{N-1}{r-1}$ possible such functions. Summing over $r = 1,\ldots, 2^d$ and imposing $r\le N$, we have
\[
\vert \hyp_d\vert = 2 \sum_{j = 0}^{\min\{2^d -1, N-1\}}\binom{N-1}{j}.
\]
Note that for $2^d > N$, 
\[
\vert \hyp_d\vert = 2 \sum_{j = 0}^{ N-1}\binom{N-1}{j} = 2^N
\]
attaining the upper bound given in Fact~\ref{fact: upp b card}. For $2^d \ll N$, instead, Stirling approximation gives
\[
\log \vert\hyp_d\vert \approx (2^d -1) \log\frac{eN}{2^d}.
\]
Thus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: log Hd}
\log \vert\hyp_d\vert \approx \min \left((2^d -1) \log\frac{eN}{2^d}, N\right). 
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Bound} We refer to the final bound as {\it Union bound}. This is a scaled version of the bound provided by Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound},
\begin{equation}\label{eq: union bound}
\mathcal B_{{Union}} (d) = C_{Union} \cdot 2\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp_d \vert}{2n}}
\end{equation}
with $C_{Union} = 0.0006$ so that $0\le \mathcal B_{Union} (d) \le 1$ for $1\le d \le 30$, the depth range we consider. See Appendix~\ref{app: code} for details on how this constant is fitted.

The first thing catching the eye is that while the generalization gap saturates from depth $20$, the union bound increases exponentially in $d$ until depth $29$. The saturating depth is only determined by $N$ and is given by roughly $\log_2 N$. 
This is shown by equation~(\ref{eq: log Hd}) or Fact~\ref{fact: upp b card}, combined with the fact that the Union bound depends on $d$ only through $\vert\hyp_d\vert$. 
Note, however, that we can choose $N$ arbitrarily large, and we use $N<\infty$ only for practical reasons. Therefore, the bound diverges until an arbitrarily large depth. 

The train and test error of binary decision trees saturate because of a specific characteristic of the algorithm. As soon as the learned tree has only ``pure'' leaves, i.e.\ leaves containing only inputs that have the same label in the training set, the algorithm ceases to split them and hence any additional allowed depth does not change the learned model. Clearly, the cardinality of the hypothesis classes $\vert\hyp_d\vert$ does not capture this property. 

Instead, a way of counting that accounts for the finiteness of our algorithm might reproduce the saturation of the test error curve. Specifically, the configuration with only pure leaves can be achieved for two reasons: either all the possible values that the inputs can take are classified, or all the inputs in the sample are classified. Since in our case $n\ll N$, the second event happens much earlier than the first one. As a consequence, a complexity measure that counts the functions in $\hyp$ {\it after\/} the algorithm has seen the data, i.e.\ that counts $h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n) : h\in \hyp$, could more faithfully represent the true complexity of the space of models that the algorithm can practically learn and yield a bound that better captures the empirical test error curve.

\section{Counting after Observing the Data}\label{sec: counting}
In this section, we introduce new tools that allow us to leverage the viewpoint exposed at the end of Section~\ref{sec: unionbound}. This allows us to obtain a saturating bound on the generalization gap. 

\subsection{Using the Rademacher Complexity}\label{sec: rademmassart}
First, we introduce the Rademacher complexity, a measure of the expressiveness of a class of functions. Then, we develop some results that build on it and yield the generalization bound. 

\begin{definition}[Empirical Rademacher complexity]\label{def: rad gen}
Let $\xi = (\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ be independent random variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. Let $\mathcal{Z} \subset \R$ be a set and $\{z_1,\ldots,z_n\}\subset \mathcal{Z}$ a collection of $n$ points. Given $\mathscr G\coloneq \{g:\mathcal{Z} \to \R\}$ a class of functions over $\mathcal{Z}$, the Rademacher complexity of $\mathscr G$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\mathscr G) \coloneq \expv \left[\sup_{g \in \mathscr G}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i g(z_i)\right]
\]
where the expectation is taken over the disorder of $\xi$. 

\textnormal{\emph{Source.} \cite{mohri2018foundations}, Definition 3.1 (empirical Rademacher complexity).}
\end{definition}

The Rademacher complexity measures the richness of a class of functions. The argument of the supremum $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\xi_i g(z_i)$ is the (scaled) inner product between $(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ and $(g(z_1),\ldots,g(z_n))$, measuring how well functions in $\mathscr G$ can align to the Rademacher variables.

In the context of our learning problem, we look at the Rademacher complexity of the class $\LL =  \{\ell_h:\X\times\Y\to\R^+ :\ell_h(x,y) = \ell(h(x),y), h \in \hyp \}$. 

\begin{definition}[Rademacher complexity of $\LL$]\label{def: rad}
Let $\xi = (\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ be independent random variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. The Rademacher complexity of the class $\LL$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \coloneq \expv \left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell_h(X_i, Y_i)\right]
\]
where the expectation is taken over the disorder of $\xi$ and $D$. 

\textnormal{\emph{Source.} \cite{mohri2018foundations}, Definition 3.2 (Rademacher complexity), applied to the loss class $\LL$.}
\end{definition}

Note that in this second definition we also take the expectation over the disorder of the data $D$, because our sample $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n)$ is random and our quantity of interest~(\ref{eq: gen gap}) is independent of the data realization. 
In this case, the argument of the supremum is the scaled inner product between the Rademacher vectors $(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ and the loss vectors $(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n))$. Since the loss function is fixed, it measures to what extent classifiers $h\in\hyp$ can align to random noise. Note that the supremum is taken before the expectation over the disorder of $\xi$ and $D$, and that it is a maximum, since $\LL$ is finite. Hence, for each dataset and for each realization of the Rademacher vectors, we can choose a different function $\ell_h \in\LL$ that attains the maximum. A high complexity means that for most values of $\xi$ we can find a model $h$ that is similar to that pattern. It is the highest when we can find a function that correlates with any possible pattern.

The following results hold. 

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher complexity bound]\label{thm: rad bound} [\cite{bach2024learning}, Proposition 4.2]
\[
\expv\left[\,\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\le 2 \mathcal R_n(\LL)
\]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} The statement and the symmetrization argument are those of~\cite{bach2024learning}, Proposition 4.2. See also~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 3.3, for the high-probability version. Here the Rademacher complexity is also averaged over the data, as in Definition~\ref{def: rad}.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}]
Let $Z\coloneq(X,Y)$ and $Z_i\coloneq(X_i,Y_i)$, so that
\[
R(h) = \expv[\ell_h(Z)] \qquad \text{and} \qquad \hat R_D(h) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_h(Z_i).
\]
Introduce an independent copy $D'=(Z_1',\ldots,Z_n')$ of the dataset $D$.
% and to study the random variables $\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)$, which have a symmetric distribution ({\it symmetrization trick}).
Since the $Z_i'$ are distributed as $Z$ and are independent of $D$, we rewrite
\[
R(h) = \expv\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_h(Z_i')\right] = \expv\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_h(Z_i')\,\Big\vert\, D\right].
\]
Then,
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}(R-\hat R_D)(h)\right] = \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL} \expv\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\,\Big\vert\, D\right]\right].
\]
since $\ell_h(Z_i)$ is a function of $D$, hence $\expv\left[\ell_h(Z_i)\,\vert\, D\right] = \ell_h(Z_i)$.
Since the supremum of expectations is at most the expectation of the supremum, and by the law of total expectation, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{app/eq: sym step1}
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}(R-\hat R_D)(h)\right] \le \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Now we apply the symmetrization trick. Let $\hyp = \{h_1,\ldots,h_m\}$, with $m = \card$. Since $Z_i$ and $Z_i'$ are i.i.d. for each $i=1,\ldots,n$, swapping them does not change the joint distribution, i.e.
\[
\left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)\right)_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} \left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')\right)_{j=1}^m.
\]
This means that, for any fixed $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\{-1,1\}^n$,
\[
\left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)\right)_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} \left(e_i\left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)\right)\right)_{j=1}^m.
\]
The pairs $(Z_1,Z_1'),\ldots,(Z_n,Z'_n)$ are also independent, therefore equality in distribution holds also when taking the sum, i.e.
\[
\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)\right)\right)_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} \left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\left(\ell_{h_j}(Z_i')-\ell_{h_j}(Z_i)\right)\right)_{j=1}^m.
\]
Finally, because the $m$ random variables are jointly equally distributed, we have equality in distribution of the suprema
\[
\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right) \overset{d}{=} \sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right).
\]
Because the two sides in the previous equation have the same distribution, their expected value is the same
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right]\, = \,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right].
\]
Note that until now we have considered only the disorder of the data $D,D'$. Because this holds for any value of $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\{-1,1\}^n$, it holds surely for the random variable $\xi = (\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$, i.e.\ 
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right]\, = \,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\bigg\vert\, \xi\right]
\]
surely. Taking the expected value over the disorder of $\xi$, we obtain
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right] = \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right]
\]
where the left hand side is independent of $\xi$ and in the right hand side we use the law of total expectation. 
Plugging the result into~(\ref{app/eq: sym step1}), we have 
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}(R-\hat R_D)(h)\right] \le\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right)\right]
\]
Now we split the sum over $i=1,\ldots,n$ into
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\left(\ell_h(Z_i')-\ell_h(Z_i)\right) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i') + \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left(-\xi_i\ell_h(Z_i)\right)
\]
Then, we use that the supremum of the sum is bounded by the sum of the suprema, 
\[
\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i') + \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left(-\xi_i\ell_h(Z_i)\right) \le \sup_{\ell_h\in\LL} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i') + \sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left(-\xi_i\ell_h(Z_i)\right)
\]
and that the expectation of the sum is the sum of the expectations, obtaining
\[
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}(R-\hat R_D)(h)\right] \le\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i')\right] + \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (-\xi_i)\ell_h(Z_i)\right]
\]
We also observe that we can replace $-\xi_i$ with $\xi_i$, because they have the same distributions, hence the same expected value, and are independent of the datasets. Therefore,
\begin{align*}
\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}(R-\hat R_D)(h)\right] &\le \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i')\right] + \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(Z_i)\right] \\
&= 2\mathcal R_n(\LL).
\end{align*}
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Massart]\label{lemma: massart} [\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 3.7]
Let $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R_+$ be a loss function bounded by $M\in\R_+$. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \vert\LL\vert}{n}}.
\]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Adapted from~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 3.7 (Massart's lemma), which is stated for a finite set of vectors $A\subset\R^n$ with $\max_{a\in A}\|a\|_2\le r$. We apply it to the loss class, where $r = M\sqrt n$, and we rewrite the proof with Hoeffding's lemma.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}]
For any $t>0$,
\begin{align}
\exp\left(t\,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right]\right)
&\le \expv\left[\exp\left(t\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\right] \notag\\
&= \expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\exp\left(t\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\right] \notag\\
&\le \expv\left[\sum_{\ell_h\in\LL}\exp\left(t\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\right] \notag\\
&= \expv\left[\sum_{\ell_h\in\LL}\prod_{i=1}^n \expv\left[\exp\left(t\xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\big\vert \,D\,\right]\right]
\label{app/eq: massart start}
\end{align}
where we use Jensen's inequality for the convex function $\exp(\cdot)$ in the first inequality, that $\exp$ is increasing in the first equality, we bound the supremum with the sum (all terms are positive) in the second inequality, and we use the independence of the $\xi_i$'s, and their independence of $D$, in the last equality.

Now we apply Hoeffding's lemma (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffdinglemma}) to each factor for fixed $(X_i,Y_i)=(x_i,y_i)$ taking any value in $\X\times\Y$. The random variable $\xi_i\ell_h(x_i,y_i)$ is centered, since $\expv[\xi_i]=0$, and it satisfies $\vert\xi_i\ell_h(x_i,y_i)\vert = \ell_h(x_i,y_i)\le M$ by non-negativity of the loss and Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}, hence it satisfies the assumptions of Hoeffding's lemma. We obtain
\[
\expv\left[\exp\left(t\xi_i\ell_h(x_i,y_i)\right)\right] \le e^{t^2M^2/2}.
\]
Because this holds for any $(x_i,y_i)\in\X\times\Y$, we have
\[
\expv\left[\exp\left(t\xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\big\vert\,D\,\right] \le e^{t^2M^2/2}
\]
surely.
Plugging this into~(\ref{app/eq: massart start}),
\[
\exp\left(t\,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right]\right) \le \expv\left[\sum_{\ell_h\in\LL}\prod_{i=1}^n e^{t^2M^2/2}\right] = \vert\LL\vert\, e^{nt^2M^2/2}.
\]
Taking the logarithm of both sides,
\[
t\,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right] \le \log\vert\LL\vert + \frac{nt^2M^2}{2},
\]
and dividing by $tn>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{app/eq: massart opt}
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le \frac{\log\vert\LL\vert}{tn} + \frac{tM^2}{2} \eqqcolon b(t)
\end{equation}
Since~(\ref{app/eq: massart opt}) holds for every $t>0$, we look for the tightest bound. We set the derivative in $t$ to zero
\[
\deriv{b(t)}{t}= 
-\frac{\log\vert\LL\vert}{t^2n} + \frac{M^2}{2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^\star = \sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\LL\vert}{nM^2}}.
\]
Observe that since it is fair to assume $\vert\LL\vert>1$, the second derivative is nonnegative for any $t$
\[\frac{2\log\vert\LL\vert}{t^3n}\ge0 \quad \forall t\] hence $t^\star$ is a minimizer (if $\vert\LL\vert = 1$, then $\log\vert\LL\vert=0$ and $b(t)=tM^2/2$ is increasing, so the bound follows by letting $t\to0^+$). Plugging $t^\star$ into~(\ref{app/eq: massart opt}) yields
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\LL\vert}{n}}
\]
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

Note that Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} can be easily extended to a bound on the gap at $\hat h_D$ because
\[
\expv[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le \expv\left[\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\]
since $\hat h_D\in\hyp$. 

Note that Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart} and Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} provide bounds with the same scaling law in $\vert \LL\vert$ and $\vert \hyp\vert$ respectively.

\begin{fact}\label{fact: L H}
  %[Equivalence with Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}]
  We have $\vert\LL\vert \le \card$ by definition of $\LL$. Moreover, if $\ell$ is the zero-one loss, the inequality is an equality, as $\ell_h$ determines $h$. 
\end{fact}

Thus, for the zero-one loss, Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} and Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart} yield the same generalization bound as Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}, up to constants. Now, we introduce a technology that allows us to take a step forward and improve this result. 

Let \[\LL(D) \coloneq \left\{\left(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)\right): h \in \hyp, D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n \right\}\] be the set of loss vectors for a random dataset $D$, i.e.\ the set containing the functions in $\LL$ applied to $D$. Let $v_i = \ell_h(X_i,Y_i)$ to ease notation. 

The supremum in Definition~\ref{def: rad} can be equivalently computed over the set of loss vectors,
\[
\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xi_i \ell_h(X_i,Y_i) = \sup_{(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\in\LL(D)} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xi_i v_i
\]

This yields the following version of the lemma. 
\begin{lemma}[Massart, loss vectors]\label{lemma: massart loss v}
Let $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R_+$ be a loss function bounded by $M\in\R_+$. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \expv[N_D(\hyp)]}{n}}
\]
where \[N_D(\hyp)\coloneq \left\vert\left\{\left(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)\right) : h\in\hyp, D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n\right\}\right\vert = \left\vert \LL(D)\right\vert\] is the cardinality of the set of loss vectors for the hypotheses in $\hyp$, which is a random variable as it depends on the dataset $D$.

\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Same argument as Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}, with the set of loss vectors $\LL(D)$ in place of $\LL$, as in~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Section 3.2 (Corollaries 3.8--3.9). The number of loss vectors is random, hence we average it over $D$.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v}]
Most of the proof is similar to Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}. 

Let $(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)) = \ell(D)$. For any $t>0$, 
\[
\exp\left(t\,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell(D) \in \LL(D)}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right]\right)\le
\expv\left[\sum_{\ell(D)\in\LL(D)}\prod_{i=1}^n \expv\left[\exp\left(t\xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\big\vert\,D\,\right]\right]
\]
following the same steps as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}, all of which are independent of the data disorder, as they can be made fixing an observation $S$. 

Now we apply Hoeffding's lemma (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffdinglemma}). These steps also regard the argument of the expectation over the Rademacher variables disorder. We obtain
\[
\expv\left[\exp\left(t\xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right)\big\vert\,D\,\right] \le e^{t^2M^2/2}.
\]
surely as in Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}. 
Plugging this into~(\ref{app/eq: massart start}),
\begin{align*}
\exp\left(t\,\expv\left[\sup_{\ell(D)\in\LL(D)}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell_h(X_i,Y_i)\right]\right) &\le \expv\left[\sum_{\ell(D)\in\LL(D)}\prod_{i=1}^n e^{t^2M^2/2}\right]\\ &= \expv\left[N_D(\hyp)e^{nt^2M^2/2}\right] \\&= \expv\left[N_D(\hyp)\right] e^{nt^2M^2/2}.
\end{align*}
The rest of the proof follows exactly as Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart}, replacing $\vert\LL\vert$ with $\expv\left[N_D(\hyp)\right]$ .
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

So, using the Rademacher complexity as an intermediate step allows us to replace the number of predictors $\card$ from Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} with the expected number of loss vectors $N_D(\hyp)$ (up to constants). 
In fact, Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} bounds a quantity that depends on the true expected value of $\ell_h(X,Y)$ with a quantity that can be computed on loss vectors. The key ingredient that allows this is a symmetrization technique, which consists in replacing the deviation of the random variable $\ell_h(X,Y)$ from its expected value with the difference between two random variables $\ell_h(X,Y)$ and $\ell_h(X',Y')$ taking two i.i.d.\ copies $D$ and $D'$ as inputs. Note that since the loss function is fixed, counting loss vectors is equivalent to counting classification vectors. We work with loss vectors here because they arise more naturally from the Rademacher complexity, and turn to classification vectors in Section~\ref{sec: growthf}. 

The following result encloses the key point of the saturation mechanism discussed at the end of Section~\ref{sec: unionbound}. 

\begin{lemma}[Counting]\label{lemma: counting} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
N_{D}(\hyp) \le \min\{\vert\hyp\vert, 2^n\} 
\]
surely.
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Elementary counting step, own formulation; it is the idea behind the growth-function bounds in~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Section 3.2.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}]
  We prove that $N_S(\hyp)\le \card$ and $N_S(\hyp)\le 2^n$ for every $S\in(\X\times\Y)^n$.
Fix $S=(z_1,\dots,z_n)$ and consider the map
\[
\Phi\colon \hyp \to {\{0,1\}}^n,\qquad
h \mapsto \bigl(\ell_h(z_1),\dots,\ell_h(z_n)\bigr),
\]
so that $N_S(\hyp)=|\Phi(\hyp)|$. Since the image of a set under any map
has cardinality at most that of the domain, $|\Phi(\hyp)|\le|\hyp|$.
Since $\Phi(\hyp)\subseteq\{0,1\}^n$, we also have
$|\Phi(\hyp)|\le|\{0,1\}^n|=2^n$. Because this holds for every realization $S$ of $D$, the claim for $N_D(\hyp)$ follows. 
\end{proof}
\end{lemma} 


All in all, we obtain the following bound on the generalization gap. 

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher and Massart bound]\label{thm: rademmassart bound} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
\expv[(R-\hat R_D) (\hat h_D)]\le \min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\card}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} 
\]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Own combination of Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}, Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} and Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}. A high-probability analogue is~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Corollaries 3.8--3.9.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: rademmassart bound}]
  Since $\hat h_S \in \hyp$ for any realization $S$ of $D$,
  \[
  \expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le \expv\left[\sup_{h\in\hyp} (R-\hat R_D)(h)\right]
  \]
  The proof is concluded applying Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}, bounding the Rademacher complexity with Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v}, and bounding $N_D(\hyp)$ with Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

We now plot the obtained bound in the context of decision trees to compare it with the empirical gap curve. 

{\bf Experimental design}

We use a heuristic normalization of the obtained bound and call this the {\it Rademacher-Massart bound},
\begin{equation}\label{eq: radmass bound}
    \mathcal B_{{RadMass}} (d) = C_{RadMass} \cdot \min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\hyp_d\vert}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} 
\end{equation} 
where $C_{RadMass} = 0.125$ so that the curve is of the same scale as the generalization gap. See Appendix~\ref{app: code} for details on how this constant is fitted. The rest of the experimental design is as above.

\subsection{Turning to the Growth Function}\label{sec: growthf}

Let us introduce the growth function, a new measure of complexity of the hypothesis class that considers classification vectors instead of loss vectors. This tool allows us to make the intuition on the saturation of the test error due to characteristics of the decision tree algorithm that we discussed in Section~\ref{sec: rademmassart} more explicit, as we count directly the distinct hypotheses in $\hyp$ after the algorithm has seen the data, instead of their loss. 

\begin{definition}[Growth function of $\hyp$]
The growth function of $\hyp$ is
\[
 \Pi_{n}(\hyp):=\sup_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X}
 \left|\left\{(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)):h\in\hyp\right\}\right|.
\]

\textnormal{\emph{Source.} \cite{mohri2018foundations}, Definition 3.3 (growth function).}
\end{definition}

The following extends Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}.

\begin{lemma}[Counting with growth function]\label{lemma: growth}
Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
 1\le N_D(\hyp)\le\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}
\]
surely. Moreover,
\[
\Pi_n(\hyp)  = N_n(\hyp) \coloneq \sup_{S\in{(\X\times\Y)}^n} N_S(\hyp)
\]
where \[ 
N_S(\hyp):= \left\vert\{(\ell_h(x_1,y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(x_n,y_n)):h\in\hyp, S = ((x_i,y_i))_{i=1}^n\}\right\vert 
\] is a realization of $N_D(\hyp)$ for a fixed dataset $S$. 

\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Own extension of Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}, relating the number of loss vectors to the growth function of~\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Section 3.2.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}]
We prove the inequalities for $N_S(\hyp)$, for every $S\in(\X\times\Y)^n$. Since $N_D(\hyp)$ takes the value $N_S(\hyp)$ when $D$ takes the value $S$, they hold for $N_D(\hyp)$ for every realization of $D$.

Fix $S=((x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n))\in(\X\times\Y)^n$, and consider the maps
\[
\Psi\colon\hyp\to\Y^n,\quad h\mapsto\bigl(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)\bigr),
\]
\[
\lambda\colon\Y^n\to\{0,1\}^n,\quad (\hat y_1,\ldots,\hat y_n)\mapsto\bigl(\ell(\hat y_1,y_1),\ldots,\ell(\hat y_n,y_n)\bigr).
\]
The map $\Phi\colon h\mapsto(\ell_h(x_1,y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(x_n,y_n))$ of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting} factorizes as $\Phi=\lambda\circ\Psi$, hence $N_S(\hyp) = |\Phi(\hyp)| = |\lambda(\Psi(\hyp))|$. Moreover, by definition of growth function, $|\Psi(\hyp)|\le\Pi_n(\hyp)$.

\emph{$1\le N_S(\hyp)$.} Since $\hyp$ is nonempty, $\Phi(\hyp)$ is nonempty.

\emph{$N_S(\hyp)\le\Pi_n(\hyp)$.} Since the image of a set under any map has cardinality at most that of the set,
\[
N_S(\hyp) = |\lambda(\Psi(\hyp))| \le |\Psi(\hyp)| \le \Pi_n(\hyp).
\]

\emph{$\Pi_n(\hyp)\le\min\{\card,2^n\}$.} Let $x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\X$ and let $\Psi$ be the corresponding map. As above, $|\Psi(\hyp)|\le\card$, and $\Psi(\hyp)\subseteq\Y^n$ with $|\Y^n| = 2^n$, since $\Y=\{-1,1\}$. Taking the supremum over $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ gives $\Pi_n(\hyp)\le\card$ and $\Pi_n(\hyp)\le2^n$.

\emph{$\Pi_n(\hyp) = N_n(\hyp)$.} Taking the supremum over $S$ in $N_S(\hyp)\le\Pi_n(\hyp)$ gives $N_n(\hyp)\le\Pi_n(\hyp)$. For the converse, let $x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\X$, take any $y_1,\ldots,y_n\in\Y$, and let $S=((x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n))$. Since $\Y=\{-1,1\}$ and $\ell$ is the zero-one loss, $\hat y_i = y_i$ if $\ell(\hat y_i,y_i)=0$ and $\hat y_i = -y_i$ if $\ell(\hat y_i,y_i)=1$. Hence $\lambda$ is injective, and
\[
|\Psi(\hyp)| = |\lambda(\Psi(\hyp))| = N_S(\hyp) \le N_n(\hyp).
\]
Taking the supremum over $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ gives $\Pi_n(\hyp)\le N_n(\hyp)$.
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}
 
The relation between $N_D$ and $\Pi_n$ makes it immediate to obtain a growth-function version of Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} (substitute $N_D(\hyp)\le\Pi_n(\hyp)$).
Substituting also $\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}$ yields exactly the bounds of Theorem~\ref{thm: rademmassart bound} and Theorem~\ref{thm: bound noiseandappr}. We continue using this measure of complexity in the next section.

\subsection{Dependence on the Best Risk}
We implement a different approach and new theoretical tools to look for a noise-sensitive bound. 

Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss for the rest of this section.

\begin{lemma}[Relative deviation]\label{lemma: rel dev}
  For $\varepsilon>0$ with $n\varepsilon^2 \ge 8$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\exists h\in\hyp : (R-\hat R_D)(h) > \varepsilon\sqrt{R(h)}\right] \le 2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/16}.
  \]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Relative deviation inequality in the spirit of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, Theorem 5.1, which relies on~\cite{bartlett1999inequality}, Section 2, Theorem 1 (see also~\cite{anthony1993result}). The statement is written for the zero-one loss with the growth function $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)$, and the constants are not optimized.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: rel dev}]
Recall $\LL=\{(x,y)\mapsto\ell(y,h(x)):h\in\hyp\}$,
where $\ell$ is the zero-one loss. Applying the first inequality of Theorem~5.1
of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, with $\mathbb{S}_\mathcal{F}(X_1^{2n}) \le N_{2n}(\hyp) = \Pi_{2n}(\hyp)$ by Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}, we obtain that with probability at least $1-\delta$, simultaneously for all
$h\in\hyp$,
\[
R(h)-\widehat R_D(h)
\le
2\sqrt{\frac{R(h)\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}}.
\]
Set
\[
\delta_\varepsilon
=
4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/4}.
\]
If $\delta_\varepsilon<1$, applying the preceding inequality
with $\delta=\delta_\varepsilon$ gives
\[
\Pr\!\left[
\exists h\in\hyp:
R(h)-\widehat R_D(h)>\varepsilon\sqrt{R(h)}
\right]
\le
4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/4}.
\]
If $\delta_\varepsilon\ge1$, this probability bound holds
trivially. Finally, since $n\varepsilon^2\ge8$,
\[
4e^{-n\varepsilon^2/4}
\le
2e^{-n\varepsilon^2/16},
\]
which proves the claim.
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

The result above is a concentration result for the random variable $\hat R_D (h)$ in terms of the true risk $R(h)$. The key ingredient in its proof is Chebyshev's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: chebyshev}) that bounds the deviation of a random variable in terms of its variance. This is a more refined result as compared to Hoeffding's inequality used in Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}, which only uses boundedness of the random variable. Moreover, when a random variable is bounded, its variance is controlled by its expected risk because, intuitively, it cannot fluctuate much around its center. In particular, for $\ell_h(X,Y)$ that is bounded and Bernoulli, we have
\[
\Var(\ell_h) = \expv[\ell_h](1-\expv[\ell_h])\le \expv[\ell_h] = R(h).
\]
This explains how we get a bound on the deviation relative to the true risk. 
The bound we obtain, moreover, depends on the growth function $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)$, i.e.\ the maximum number of classification vectors for datasets of size $2n$. This quantity arises naturally because the result uses a symmetrization technique. Also, as presented in Section~\ref{sec: rademmassart}, this technique considers two independent datasets $D$ and $D'$ and we count loss vectors over their union, hence the size $2n$ instead of $n$. 

In high probability, and isolating the empirical risk $\hat R_D$ on the right hand side, we obtain the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Bound with empirical risk]\label{thm: bound emprisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{\hat R_D(\hat h_D)\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 16\frac{\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Analogous to~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, Theorem 5.1, rewritten to isolate $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$ by solving a quadratic inequality, starting from Lemma~\ref{lemma: rel dev}.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}]
Let $\delta\in(0,1)$ and set
\[
u\coloneq\frac{\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \varepsilon\coloneq 4\sqrt{u},
\]
so that $2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/16} = \delta$. Since $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)\ge1$ and $\delta<1$, we have $n\varepsilon^2 = 16\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta) \ge 16\log 2 \ge 8$, hence Lemma~\ref{lemma: rel dev} applies and gives
\[
\Pr\left[\exists h\in\hyp : (R-\hat R_D)(h) > \varepsilon\sqrt{R(h)}\right] \le \delta.
\]
Therefore, with probability at least $1-\delta$, we have $(R-\hat R_D)(h)\le\varepsilon\sqrt{R(h)}$ for all $h\in\hyp$, and in particular for $h=\hat h_D\in\hyp$.

On this event, we isolate $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$. Let $r\coloneq R(\hat h_D)$ and $\hat r\coloneq\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$, so that $r-\varepsilon\sqrt r-\hat r\le0$. This is a quadratic inequality in $x\coloneq\sqrt r\ge0$, namely $x^2-\varepsilon x-\hat r\le0$, which implies that $x$ is at most the largest root,
\[
\sqrt r \le \frac{\varepsilon+\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4\hat r}}{2}.
\]
Squaring both sides,
\[
r \le \frac{2\varepsilon^2 + 4\hat r + 2\varepsilon\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4\hat r}}{4} = \hat r + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4\hat r} \le \hat r + \varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon\sqrt{\hat r},
\]
where in the last inequality we used $\sqrt{a+b}\le\sqrt a+\sqrt b$. Hence, substituting $\varepsilon = 4\sqrt u$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{align*}
(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D) &= r-\hat r \le \varepsilon\sqrt{\hat r} + \varepsilon^2 \\ &= 4\sqrt{\frac{\hat R_D(\hat h_D)\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 16\frac{\log(2\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}.
\end{align*}
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

We now want to bound the empirical risk of the ERM $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$ in the right-hand side of the result above. 

Let
\[
R^*_\hyp = \min_{h\in\hyp} R(h)
\]
be the minimum risk attainable in the hypotheses set
and let \[
h_b \in \argmin_{h\in\hyp} R(h)
\]
be the best hypothesis, i.e.\ the one attaining $R^*_\hyp$. Again, at least one such model exists since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty. 

By Bernstein's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: bernstein}) on $\hat R_D(h_b)$ and the definition of ERM (equation~(\ref{eq: ERM})), we obtain the following result. 

\begin{corollary}[Bound with best risk]\label{coroll: bound bestrisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_{\hyp}\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Own result; for a related ERM excess-risk bound depending on the best risk in the class see~\cite{bartlett1999inequality}, Section 3, Theorem 2. Here we bound instead the generalization gap, using Bernstein's inequality on $\hat R_D(h_b)$ and Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk}]
        Recall $h_b = \argmin_{h\in\hyp} R(h)$, $R(h_b) = R^*_{\hyp}$ by definition of $R^*_\hyp$ and $\hat R_D(\hat h_D) \le \hat R_D(h_b)$ by definition of the ERM $\hat h_D$.
        Since $h_b$ is independent of the data, the variables $\ell(h_b(X_i),Y_i)$ are i.i.d.\ Bernoulli with mean $R^*_\hyp$ and variance $R^*_\hyp(1-R^*_\hyp) \le R^*_\hyp$. Bernstein's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: bernstein}) with $M=1$ gives, with probability at least $1-\delta/2$,
        \[
        \hat R_D(h_b) \le R^*_{\hyp} + \sqrt{\frac{2R^*_\hyp \log (2/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{3n}.
        \]
        Let $u\coloneq\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)/n$. Since $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)\ge 1$, $\log(2/\delta)/n\le u$. Thus
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: bound hatR h0}
        \hat R_D(\hat h_D) \le \hat R_D(h_b) \le R^*_\hyp + 2\sqrt{R^*_\hyp u} + u = {\left(\sqrt{R^*_\hyp} + \sqrt{u}\right)}^2.
        \end{equation}
        Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk} at confidence $1-\delta/2$ gives, with probability at least $1-\delta/2$,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: bo}
        (R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\hat R_D(\hat h_D)\,u} + 16u.
        \end{equation}
        By a union bound, both events hold simultaneously with probability at least $1-\delta$. Hence, plugging~(\ref{eq: bound hatR h0}) in the RHS of~(\ref{eq: bo}), we obtain that with probability at least $1-\delta$,
        \begin{align*}
        (R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D) &\le 4\sqrt u\left(\sqrt{R^*_\hyp} + \sqrt u\right) + 16u \\
        &= 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_{\hyp}\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}.
        \end{align*}
\end{proof}
\end{corollary}

The result above yields a bound on the generalization gap that depends on the best risk. The key to obtaining this dependence is that Bernstein's is a concentration result in terms of the variance, like Chebyshev's, and $\Var(\ell_{h_b}) \le \expv[\ell_{h_b}] = R^*_\hyp$. 

We now make the dependence on the noise explicit. 

Let
\[
R^* := \inf_{h:\X\to\Y} R(h)
\] 
be the Bayes risk. Note that the infimum in the definition runs over a superset of $\hyp$, hence $R^* \le R^*_\hyp$. The best risk can be decomposed as 
\[
R^*_\hyp = \underbrace{R^*}_{\text{Bayes risk}} + \underbrace{R^*_\hyp - R^*}_{\text{Approximation error}} =: R^* + A_\hyp
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
\item the Bayes risk $R^*$ measures the classification error due to the irreducible noise $\varepsilon$, as it is the minimal error that any function $h:\X\to\Y$ necessarily makes;
\item the approximation error $A_\hyp$ is the excess error we make given that we consider a restricted set of predictors, $\hyp$.
\end{itemize}
Note that if there is no noise, then $R^* = 0$ and the Bayes risk is attained by the sign of the data-generating function $\sgn \circ f^*$. Moreover, if the model attaining $R^*$ lives in the hypothesis class, then $A_\hyp = 0$.  

We obtain the following result. 

\begin{corollary}\label{coroll: bound noiseandappr}
With probability $1-\delta$,
\[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{( R^*+A_\hyp)\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
\]
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Own result: it follows from Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk} and the decomposition of the best risk into Bayes risk (see~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, Section 2) and approximation error.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound noiseandappr}]
  Substitute $R^*_\hyp = R^* + A_\hyp$ into Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk}.
\end{proof}
\end{corollary}

Tail integrating and using the counting lemma (Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}) yield the following theorem. 

\begin{theorem}[Noise and approximation error bound]\label{thm: bound noiseandappr}
  Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le \min\Bigg\{ &4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\left(\log (4\card)+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\card)+1}{n}, \\
&4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\left((2n+2)\log 2+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{(2n+2)\log 2+1}{n} \Bigg\}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\textnormal{\emph{Source.} Own result: it follows from Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound noiseandappr} by tail integration (Proposition~\ref{app: tail int}) and Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: bound noiseandappr}]
    Let $\text{gap} = (R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)$ and $C = \sqrt{\frac{\log(4 \Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}}$. Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk} gives
\begin{align*}
&\Pr\left[\text{gap} \le 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C + 20C^2\right] \ge 1-\delta \\
\implies &\Pr\left[\text{gap} > 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C + 20C^2\right] \le \delta
\end{align*}
Set $\varepsilon = 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C + 20C^2$. Let us now find $\delta(\varepsilon)$:
\[
20C^2 + 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C - \varepsilon = 0 \implies C = \frac{-4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp} + \sqrt{16R^*_\hyp + 80\varepsilon}}{40} = \frac{\sqrt{R^*_\hyp + 5\varepsilon} - \sqrt{R^*_\hyp}}{10}
\]
So we have
\begin{align*}
\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n} &= \left(\frac{\sqrt{R^*_\hyp + 5\varepsilon} - \sqrt{R^*_\hyp}}{10}\right)^2 \\
\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta} &= \exp\left(n\left(\frac{\sqrt{R^*_\hyp + 5\varepsilon} - \sqrt{R^*_\hyp}}{10}\right)^2\right)
\end{align*}
and hence
\[
\delta(\varepsilon) = 4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)\exp\left(-\frac{n}{100}\left(\sqrt{R^*_\hyp + 5\varepsilon} - \sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\right)^2\right).
\]
Using that $\expv[X] = \int_0^\infty \Pr[X \ge \varepsilon]\,d\varepsilon$ for $X \ge 0$, applied to $\text{gap}^+ \coloneq \max\{\text{gap}, 0\}$,
\[
\expv[\text{gap}] \le \expv[\text{gap}^+] = \int_0^\infty \Pr[\text{gap} \ge \varepsilon]\,d\varepsilon \le \int_0^\infty \min\{1, \delta(\varepsilon)\}\,d\varepsilon.
\]
We now compute the integral by exchanging the roles of $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$. The map $\varepsilon\mapsto\delta(\varepsilon)$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $[0,\infty)$, with $\delta(0) = 4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp) > 1$ and $\delta(\varepsilon)\to0$ as $\varepsilon\to\infty$. Hence, for every $t\in(0,1)$ there is a unique $\varepsilon(t)>0$ such that $\delta(\varepsilon(t)) = t$, and it is given by $\varepsilon(t) = 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C + 20C^2$ with $C = \sqrt{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)/t)/n}$, by construction of $\delta(\varepsilon)$. Moreover, for $t\in(0,1)$,
\[
\min\{1,\delta(\varepsilon)\} > t \iff \delta(\varepsilon) > t \iff \varepsilon < \varepsilon(t).
\]
Writing $\min\{1,\delta(\varepsilon)\} = \int_0^1 \mathbf 1\{t < \min\{1,\delta(\varepsilon)\}\}\,dt$ and exchanging the order of integration by Tonelli's theorem (the integrand is non-negative), we obtain
\[
\int_0^\infty \min\{1, \delta(\varepsilon)\}\,d\varepsilon = \int_0^1 \int_0^\infty \mathbf 1\{\varepsilon < \varepsilon(t)\}\,d\varepsilon\,dt = \int_0^1 \varepsilon(t)\,dt = \int_0^1 \varepsilon(\delta)\,d\delta.
\]
Hence, writing $\varepsilon(\delta) = 4\sqrt{R^*_\hyp}\,C + 20C^2$ explicitly,
\[
\expv[\text{gap}] \le \int_0^1 \varepsilon(\delta)\,d\delta = 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_\hyp}{n}}\int_0^1 \sqrt{\log\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta}}\,d\delta + \frac{20}{n}\int_0^1 \log\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta}\,d\delta.
\]
The logarithm is the only part depending on $\delta$, and its integral is
\[
\int_0^1 \log\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta}\,d\delta = \log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) - \int_0^1 \log\delta\,d\delta = \log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) + 1.
\]
Applying Jensen's inequality to the square root term,
\[
\int_0^1 \sqrt{\log\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta}}\,d\delta \le \sqrt{\int_0^1 \log\frac{4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{\delta}\,d\delta} = \sqrt{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) + 1},
\]
we obtain
\[
\expv[\text{gap}] \le 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_\hyp\left(\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) + 1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) + 1}{n}.
\]
The right-hand side is increasing in $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)$. Hence, substituting $R^*_\hyp = R^*+A_\hyp$ and $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\card,2^{2n}\}$ (Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth} with $2n$ in place of $n$), and noting that $\log(4\cdot 2^{2n}) = (2n+2)\log 2$, we obtain the claim.

Asymptotically,
\[
\expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le O\left(
\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\,\log \Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{n}} + \frac{\log \Pi_{2n}(\hyp)}{n} \right)
\]
because, at the numerator,
\[
\log (4 \Pi_{2n}(\hyp)) + 1 = \log 4 + \log \Pi_{2n}(\hyp) + 1 = \log \Pi_{2n}(\hyp) + O(1)
\]
and for large $x$, 
\[
\log x + O(1) \le C \log x
\] 
for some constant $C>0$.
Applying $\Pi_{2n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\card,2^{2n}\}$ (Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}), we obtain
\[
\expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le \min\left\{ O\left(
\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\log \card}{n}} + \frac{\log\card}{n} \right), O\left(\sqrt{R^*+A_{\hyp}}+1 \right)\right\}
\]
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

The result above is a bound on the expected gap at the ERM that explicitly depends on the noise $R^*$ and that uses a measure of complexity that counts hypotheses after the algorithm has seen the data. 
% We have obtained it by using more advanced concentration inequalities that take into account the variance of the random variable, while Hoeffding's inequality and Hoeffding's lemma used in Section~\ref{sec: unionbound} and~\ref{sec: rademmassart} are myopic to the distribution of the random variable and only use that it ranges over a bounded interval. Also, the result is specific to the ERM $\hat h_D$, whose empirical risk can be bounded in terms of the best risk. The best risk achievable by our hypotheses, in turn, is at least equal to the risk due to irreducible noise in the data.
We plot it and investigate how it relates to the empirical error curve of decision trees. Note that Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk} uses the definition of ERM. Therefore, strictly speaking the bound obtained in Theorem~\ref{thm: bound noiseandappr} does not hold for the model selected by the DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm used in the experiments. Nonetheless, we present the comparison between the two curves for explorative purposes.

{\bf Experimental design}

As compared to Sections~\ref{sec: unionbound} and~\ref{sec: rademmassart} that used a Gaussian noise, we switch to a label-flip noise with flip probability $1-p$. This makes the computation of the Bayes risk and of the best risk easier. Therefore, we generate the labels as 
\[
y \leftarrow \sgn(f^*(x))\cdot b
\]
where $f^*$ is the sine function~(\ref{eq: sine}) and $b$ is a random variable taking value $1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability $1-p$. We use $p\in\{0.6,0.8,0.95\}$.

Since $p\ge 0.5$, the Bayes risk is 
\[
R^* = 1-p.
\]
This is because the hypothesis attaining the Bayes risk selects the most probable label for each input ({\it Bayes classifier\/} in Section 2 of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}). When $\Pr(\text{flip})=1-p<0.5$, the most probable label is the noiseless one determined by $\sgn(f^*(x))$, which gives error $1-p$. The best risk $R^*_{\hyp_d}$ is computed exactly for each $d$ using dynamic programming (see Section~\ref{app: bestrisk} in the Appendix for details on the computation of the best risk). 

As always, we plot a normalized version of the bound obtained in Theorem~\ref{thm: bound noiseandappr}, which we call {\it Noise bound}: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: noise bound}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal B_{Noise}(d) = \,&C_{Noise} \cdot \min\Bigg\{ 4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*_{\hyp_d})\left(\log (4\card)+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\card)+1}{n}, \\
    &4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*_{\hyp_d})\left((2n+2)\log 2+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{(2n+2)\log 2+1}{n} \Bigg\}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with $C_{Noise} = 0.01$ so that the curve is of the same scale as the generalization gap. See Appendix~\ref{app: code} for details on how this constant is fitted.

Figure~\ref{fig: noise bound} shows the Noise bound $\mathcal B_{Noise}$ from equation~(\ref{eq: noise bound}) against the empirical curve of the generalization gap. The curve is similar to that of the Rademacher-Massart bound (Figure~\ref{fig: radmass}), with the bound generally increasing at smaller depths before reaching a plateau. Hence, the Noise bound also captures the saturation of the gap, as expected thanks to the counting lemma. Moreover, Figure~\ref{fig: noise bound many noise} shows that the bound moves for different levels of the noise, capturing the sensitivity of the empirical error that we have seen at the beginning of this section. The saturating value is higher for higher levels of noise, as happens to the generalization gap. At the same time, the change in the theoretical curve is way smaller compared to the empirical one, which constitutes a limitation to the descriptive power of this curve.
 
\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{outputs/best_risk_many_noise.png}
  \caption{}\label{fig: best risk many noise}
\end{figure}

 Finally, we aim at obtaining a better understanding of this curve. The Noise bound changes with the noise because it is a function of the best risk $R^*_\hyp$. Figure~\ref{fig: best risk many noise} allows us to investigate how $R^*_\hyp$ is affected by different noise levels (pink line). Let $d_c$ be the depth at which the best risk saturates, which is the same in the three graphs. We make two observations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(1)] for $d< d_c$, the best risk decreases faster with less noise;
    \item[(2)] for $d\ge d_c$, the best risk saturates and the saturating level is lower with less noise. 
\end{enumerate}
The first observation follows from the label-flip model: predicting the noiseless label incurs error (1-p), whereas predicting its opposite incurs error (p). Correcting the classification of a region therefore reduces the risk by its probability mass multiplied by (2p-1). Consequently, the improved approximation of the noiseless classification pattern allowed by increasing depth produces a larger reduction in the best risk when there is less noise.
The second observation is directly determined by the irreducible noise in the data. In fact, the best risk saturates exactly at the Bayes risk (black, dashed line).

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec: concl}

This work has inquired and qualitatively validated with experiments classical generalization bounds for decision trees. We have started from a bound using Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over the hypotheses set, which did not capture the saturation of the empirical error curve. Then, we have taken into account that measuring the complexity of the class of functions that decision trees can learn after they have seen the data is more representative of how the algorithm works in practice. By including this in our analysis via Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}, we have obtained a saturating bound. Finally, 
we have noticed that the generalization performance of decision trees is affected by the level of noise in the data. Thus, using more refined concentration inequalities, we have obtained a bound that is a function of the lowest risk that a decision tree can attain, that depends on the noise through the Bayes risk. This, combined with the result on the actual complexity of the hypotheses space, has given a bound that saturates and that is sensitive to the noise. 

The main limitation of this thesis is the lack of an analysis of the difference between the selected model that we consider for theoretical results, i.e.\ the Empirical Risk Minimizer, and the model learned in practice by the DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm. In fact, this is a greedy algorithm that does not always select the best possible model. Among the results we derived, only the lower bound in Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} and the results from Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk} onwards are specific to the ERM. Analyzing more in depth this criticality might be a direction for future work. 

Secondly, although we obtain a bound that saturates and is noise-sensitive, there is clearly space for improving this study and obtaining functions that capture the qualitative behavior of the generalization gap also under other aspects. In this regard, we believe that a promising direction is to study the qualitative descriptive power of bounds based on a {\it localized\/} version of the Rademacher complexity, proposed by~\cite{Bartlett_2005}. Another potential development is to consider other measures of complexity of the hypothesis class, not necessarily based on the maximum depth. 

Finally, we hope that the framework and approach proposed in this work might be applied to other algorithms in the future. Specifically, a natural next step would be Random Fourier Features, while we propose neural networks trained with Stochastic Gradient Descent as a more ambitious one.
