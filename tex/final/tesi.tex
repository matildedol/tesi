{\bf Setting}

Given a sample space $\Omega$, let inputs and labels be generated by $X:\Omega \to \mathcal X = \{\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_N\}$ and $Y: \Omega \to \mathcal Y = \{-1, 1\}$. Let $p$ be the joint probability distribution of $(X,Y)$ on $\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y$, with
\[
 p_{j,y}=\Pr[X=\bar x_j,Y=y],\qquad
 p_{j,y}\ge0,\qquad
 \sum_{j=1}^N\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}p_{j,y}=1.
\]
Let $D = {(X_i, Y_i)}_{i=1}^n$ be a dataset of $n$ indipendent copies of $(X,Y)$ with common distribution $p$. We use the term {\it dataset\/} to refer to the {\it random variable\/} generating the data throughout this work. We denote by $S = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n$ a realization of it. In learning theory, we assume to observe $S$ and hope to learn a function that given a new input $x\in\X$ classifies it correctly according to $p$. 
Let $\hyp \subseteq \{h:\mathcal X \to \R\}$ be a nonempty, finite set of predictors where we look for such a function.
Given a loss function $\ell:\R \times \mathcal Y \to \mathbb R^+$, define the population and empirical risks by
\[
    R(h) = \mathbb E_D(\ell(h(X), Y)) = \sum_{\bar x\in\mathcal X} \sum_{\bar y \in\{-1,1\}} \ell(h(\bar x), \bar y) p(\bar x, \bar y).
\]
and
\[
    \hat R_D (h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(X_i), Y_i).
\]
Because we do not have access to $p$, we select the predictor that minimizes the empirical risk (ERM), i.e.
\begin{equation*}\label{eq: ERM}
    \hat h_D \in \argmin_{h\in \hyp} \hat R_D (h)
\end{equation*}
which, importantly, is a random variable of the data $D$. Note that at least one such $\hat h_D$ exists, since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty.

{\bf Approach}

We study bounds on the expcted gap between the test and train error of the ERM, \begin{equation} \label{eq: target}
\expv_D\left[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right]
\end{equation}
Note that since $\hat h_D$ and $\hat R_D$ depend on the same dataset $D$, $\expv_D[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)]\neq R(\hat h_D)$, i.e.\ the empirical risk is a biased estimate of the true risk. A proof of this fact is found in Appendix~\ref{app: add res}. % non è vero ehhehee la devo ancora fare

This approach allows to obtain bounds on the risk of the learnt model $\hat h_D$ instead of uniform bounds over the whole space of hypotheses as usual in learning theory, as the algorithm may not explore it~\cite{bousquet2002stability}. Moreover, the training error usually goes to $0$ in our setting where the dataset is smaller than the input space, hence a bound on~(\ref{eq: target}) corresponds to a bound on the test error for a large enough depth. In light of this, we refer to a bound on the gap as a bound on the test error throughout this work.
We detach from the usual literature also in that we express concentration results as bounds on the expected value of the target quantity via tail integration, rather than using results in high probability or bounding the probability that this quantity is large. In fact, results in expected value are more practical for the experiments and their interpretation is clearer as compared to high-probability results. 

While some results are stated in a general setting, for arbitrary hypothesis classes and loss functions, we develop our main results for binary decision trees under the zero-one loss. Specifically, 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the zero-one loss is $\ell(h(x), y) = \mathbf{1} (h(x)\neq y) = \frac{1 - h(x)y}{2}$;
    \item we denote by $\hyp_d$ the set of binary decision trees $h:\X\to\Y$ with depth at most $d$ for $d>0$, and we let $d$ range over increasing values. 
\end{itemize}

Each one of the following sections is organized as follows. First, we present theoretical results in the most generic case given the setting above. Then, we describe the experimental design and state the final asymptotic bound, which we then plot alongside an empirical estimate of~(\ref{eq: target}) to assess how well it captures the qualitative behavior of the test error curve. We conclude commenting on the results.

\section{Union bound over $\hyp$}\label{sec: unionbound}
In this section, we present a fundamental result on the behavior of the gap between the expected and true risk of the empirical risk minimizer. First, we make the following assumption.

\begin{assumption}[Boundedness of loss function]\label{ass: bounded loss}
    Given a set $A\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\to\R^+$,
    $\exists M\in\R$ s.t.
    \[
    \ell (a) \le M \qquad \forall a\in A.
    \]
\end{assumption}

A bounded loss function is crucial for obtaining all the upper bounds on the risk discussed in this work. However, the following holds.

\begin{assumption}[Lipschitzness of loss function and bound at zero]\label{ass: lipsch loss}
Given a set $A\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\to\R^+$, 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\exists C>0$ s.t.
        \[ \forall a,a^\prime\in A, \vert \ell(a)-\ell(a^\prime)\vert \le C\vert a-a^\prime\vert\]

        \item $\exists C^\prime >0$ s.t.
        \[ \vert \ell(0)\vert \le C^\prime
        \]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop: suff assump 2.2}
    For $A\coloneqq \{a\in\R : \vert a \vert \le B\text{ for some constant } B \in \R\}$, Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss} $\Rightarrow$ Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss}. 
\end{proposition}

Note that, in general, we can always take a {\it\/truncation map} $T:\R\to[-1,1]$, defined as 
\begin{equation}
    T(a) = \begin{cases}
        -1 & a \le -1\\
        a & -1<a<1, \qquad\qquad a\in A\\
        1 & a\ge 1.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This allows to set $\hyp_T =  T\circ \hyp$ and take $A = \{(h(x),y) : h\in\hyp_T, x\in\X, y\in\Y = \{1,-1\}\}$ which satisfies the assumption of bounded inputs required by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. 

Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2} combined with the truncation map argument allows to reduce the requirement of boundedness to that of Lipschitzness of the loss function. The latter does not depend on the hypotheses set and is sometimes easier to obtain, as Example~\ref{ex: logistic} shows. Also, this provides a framework for extending the results in this work to an arbitrary $\hyp$, possibly infinite, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec: concl}.   

\begin{example}[Logistic loss function]\label{ex: logistic}
    Let $\ell (a) = \log (1+e^{-a}), a \in \R$ be the logistic loss function. Note that in our setting the function takes as input the product $h(x)\cdot y$.
    We have
    \[
    \ell'(a) = -\frac{e^{-a}}{1+e^{-a}} = -\frac{1}{1+e^a}\quad \Rightarrow \quad \vert \ell'(a)\vert < 1 \qquad\forall a \in \R
    \]
    hence 
    \[
    C = \sup_{a\in\R} \vert \ell^\prime\vert \le 1.
    \]
    Also,
    \[
    \ell(0) = \log 2 = C^\prime.
    \]
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
  The zero-one loss satisfies Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss} with $M=1$ by definition. 
\end{remark}

We are ready to state our first result. We obtain the upper bound starting from an exponential concentration result (Hoeffding's inequality, Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffding} in the Appendix) applied to the random variable $\hat R_D(h)$ for a fixed function $h\in\hyp$. We then obtain the result at $\hat h_D$ through a union bound (Proposition~\ref{app: unionbound} in the Appendix). This is because $\hat h_D$ is not fixed as it depends on the data $D$, hence we need to bound the probability that the event holds for any function $h\in\hyp$. This justifies the dependence on $\card$. Finally, we tail integrate to obtain a bound on the expected value, which yields the logarithmic rate. 

\begin{theorem}\label{thm: unionbound} Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. Then
\[
  0 \le \expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le 2M\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp \vert}{2n}}
\]
\end{theorem}

The result shows that the empirical risk of the empirical risk minimizer underestimates its true risk and it provides a bound on the gap that depends on the square root of the logarithm of the cardinality of the hypotheses space. We now describe the experimental design for plotting this bound in the case of binary decision trees. 

{\bf Experimental design}

The preliminary experiments mentioned below are described and their plots are shown in Appendix~\ref{app: prel exp}. 

We create the support $\mathcal X$ as a discretization over $N= 10,000$ numbers of the interval $[0,10]$. We sample from it with the uniform measure $p_\X(\bar x) = \sum_{\bar y\in \Y}p(\bar x, \bar y) = \frac{1}{N}$. 
The law to generate the labels is
\[
y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}(h^*(x) + \sigma\varepsilon)
\] 
where $h^*:\X\to\Y$, is the data-generating function, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, is a Gaussian noise, and $\sigma>0$. 
First, we generate the labels with a centered parabola, so that roughly half of them are $1$ and half $-1$ in the interval $[0,10]$. 
Since an algorithm could learn such a function with few datapoints (see Section~\ref{app/ sec: datagen}), we switch to a sinusoid with varying frequency,
\[
    h^*(x) = 5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right)
\]
where $m$ is the number of times $h^*$ crosses the $x$-axis in the open interval $(0,10)$, yielding $m+1$ contiguous blocks of $1s$ and $-1s$. 
We set as default value $m=10$.

We test a Bernoullian noise instead of a Gaussian one, concluding that there is no interesting difference in the performance of the algorithm as long as the portion of misclassified inputs due to the noise is the same. We find that for $m = 10$, $\sigma = 3.5$ achieves a noise of $20\%$, corresponding to $p =0.8$. For now, we stick to a gaussian noise.

In the end, our labels are generated as
 \[
    y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}\left(5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right) + \sigma\varepsilon\right)
 \]
 with $\sigma = 3.5$ and $m = 10$. Also, our $\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)$ maps $0$ to $1$. 

We proceed as follows. We generate a test set with size $n_\text{test} = 200,000$ sampling from $\X$ with $p_\X$ and with replacement, and generating the labels as above. Then, for each depth $d \in [1, 7\lceil{\log_2 n}\rceil] \subset \mathbb N$ we do the following. For a number $T=100$ of iterations, we 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\cdot$] generate a train set ($n= 50$);
    \item[$\cdot$] fit a decision tree with depth at most $d$ (DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm from the scikit-learn library);
    \item[$\cdot$] compute the train error, the test error, and the differene between the two (the gap).
\end{itemize}
Then, we average the resulting quantities over the $T$ samples, obtaining empirical estimates of the expected values of the train error, test error, and gap over the disorder of the data. 

To plot the theoretical bound, instead, we need to express $\vert \hyp_d\vert$ as a function of $d$.
The exact computation gives
\[
\vert \hyp_d \vert = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\min{N-1, 2^d-1}} \binom{N-1}{k}
\]

\expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_{D,d})] \le O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp_d \vert}{n}}\right) \qquad \text{for }d>0.

We plot the bound~(\ref{eq: union bound}) in Figure~\ref{fig:pic1}. The first thing catching the eye is that the bound diverges, while the test error saturates. This divergence is due to the dependence of the bound on $\vert\hyp_d\vert$, that is the number of binary trees with maximum depth $d$, which clearly increases with $d$. It follows immediately that a complexity measure of the hypotheses set that naively counts the functions it contains does not satisfy our requirements to find a qualitatively informative bound.

The test error of binary decision trees saturates because of a specific characteristic of the algorithm. As soon as the learned tree has only ``pure'' leaves, i.e.\ leaves containing only inputs that have the same label in the training set, the algortihm ceases to split them and hence any additional allowed depth does not change the learned model. Clearly, the cardinality of the hypotheses sets $\hyp_d, d>0$ does capture this property. 
% devo definire sopra cos'è H bene e H_d
Instead, a counting way that accounts for the finiteness of our problem might reproduce the saturation of the test error curve. Specifically, the configuration with only pure leaves can be achieved for two reasons: either all the possible values that the inputs can take are classified, or all the inputs in the sample are classified. Since, in our case, $n\ll N$, the second event happens much earlier than the first one. As a consequence, a complexity measure that counts the number of distinct classifications that the functions in $\hyp$ can give, i.e.\ the vectors $h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n) : h\in \hyp$, instead of the functions themselves, could more faithfully represent the true complexity of the space of models that the algorithm can learn in practice.

\section{Using the Rademacher complexity}\label{sec: rademmassart}
In this section, we introduce the Rademacher complexity, a tool that allows to leverage the viewpoint exposed in the last section and obtain a saturating bound on the test error of decision trees. As before, we state the first results for generic loss functions and hypotheses sets.  

Define $\LL =  \{\ell_h : h \in \hyp \}$ and $\ell_h(x,y) = \ell(h(x), y)$. 

\begin{definition}[Rademacher complexity]\label{def: rad}
Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ be independent random variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. The Rademacher complexity of the set $\LL$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \coloneq \expv_{\xi,D} \left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell_h(X_i, Y_i)\right]
\]
\end{definition}

The Rademacher complexity measures the richness of a class of functions. The argument of the supremum  $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\xi_i \ell_h(X_i,Y_i) = \langle \xi, \ell_h(X,Y)\rangle$ is the correlation between $\xi$ and $\ell_h(X,Y)$. Since the loss function is fixed, this correlation measures to what extent the predictors can align to random labels. Note that the supremum is taken before the expectation over the disorder of $\xi$ and $D$, and that in our case it is a maximum, since $\LL$ is finite. Hence, for each dataset and for each realization of the Rademacher vectors, we can choose a different function $\ell_h \in\LL$ that attains the maximum. A high complexity means that for most values of $\xi$ we can find a model $h$ that is similar to that pattern. It is the highest when we can find a function that correlates with any possible pattern.

The following results hold. 

Define $N_D(\hyp)=\left|\left\{(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)):h\in\hyp\right\}\right|$.

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher complexity bound]\label{thm: rad bound} [\cite{bach2024learning}, Proposition 4.2]
\[
\expv_D\left[\,\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\le 2 \mathcal R_n(\LL)
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Massart]\label{lemma: massart} [\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 3.3]
Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \card}{n}}
\]
\end{lemma}

Note that Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} can be easily extended to a bound on the gap at $\hat h_D$ because
\[
\expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le \expv_D\left[\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\]
since $\hat h_D\in\hyp$. Moreover, the supremum in Definition~\ref{def: rad} can be equivalently computed over the set of loss vectors, which yields the following version of the lemma. 

\begin{lemma}[Massart, loss vectors]\label{lemma: massart loss v}
Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le \expv_D\left[M\sqrt{\frac{2\log N_D(\hyp)}{n}}\right]
\]
\end{lemma}

So, using the Rademacher complexity as an intermediate step allows us to replace the number of predictors $\card$ from Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} with the number of loss vectors $N_D(\hyp)$ (up to constants). 
In fact, Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} bounds a quantity that depends on the true expected value of $\ell_h(X,Y)$ with a quantity that can be computed on loss vectors. The key ingredient for proving it is a symmetrization technique, which consists in replacing the deviation of the random variable $\ell_h(X,Y)$ from its expected value with the difference between two identical random variables taking independent datasets $D$ and $D'$ as inputs. Note that since the loss function is fixed, counting loss vectors is equivalent to counting classification vectors. We work with loss vectors here because they arise more naturally from Rademacher complexity, and turn to classification vectors in Section~\ref{sec: growthf}. 

The following result encloses the key point of the saturation mechanism discussed at the end of Section~\ref{sec: unionbound}. 

\begin{lemma}[Counting inequalities]\label{lemma: counting} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
N_{D}(\hyp) \le \min\{\vert\hyp\vert, 2^n\} 
\]
surely.
\end{lemma} 

Substituting this bound on $N_D$ in Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} and combining it with Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}, we obtain the following bound. 

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher and Massart bound]\label{thm: rademmassart bound} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
\expv_D[(R-\hat R_D) (\hat h_D)]\le \min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\card}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} 
\]
\end{theorem}

The experimental design is the same as above. Again, we ignore the constants and use
\begin{equation}\label{eq: rademmassart bound}
\min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\card}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} = \min\left\{O\left(\sqrt\frac{\log\card}{n}\right), O(1)\right\}.
\end{equation}
We set the constant term to $0.5$, because the Rademacher complexity of $\LL$ is naturally bounded by $0.5$ (see Appendix~\ref{app: add res} for a proof of this fact).

\section{Dependence on the best risk}\label{sec: bestrisk}
In this section, we implement a different approach and new theoretical tools to look for a noise-sensitive bound.

Let the minimum expected risk achievable in the hypotheses set be
\[
R^*_\hyp = \min_{h\in\hyp} R(h).
\]
We aim at obtaining a bound on the test error of $\hat h_D$ in terms of the best risk $R^*_\hyp$. The first ingredient is a concentration result for $\hat R_D$ that depends on the true risk $R$. The key ingredient is Chebyshev inequality, Theorem~\ref{app: chebyshev} in the Appendix. 

Define $N_{n}(\hyp) := \sup_{S \in {(\X \times \Y)}^n} N_S(\hyp)$. Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss for the rest of this section.

\begin{lemma}[Relative deviation]\label{lemma: rel dev}
  For $\varepsilon>0$ with $n\varepsilon^2 \ge 8$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\exists h\in\hyp : (R-\hat R_D)(h) > \varepsilon\sqrt R(h)\right] \le 2N_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/16}.
  \]
\end{lemma}

The dependence on the supremum of the number of loss vectors $N_n$ emerges naturally, since the proof of this result uses a symmetrization technique. Using the supremum instead of the expectation over $D$ as in Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} is only a trick to avoid handling the dependence on the disorder of the dataset. 

In high probability, and isolating the empirical risk $\hat R_D$ on the right hand side, we obtain the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Bound with empirical risk]\label{thm: bound emprisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{\hat R_D(\hat h_D)\log(2N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 16\frac{\log(2N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\end{theorem}

Let
\[
h_0 \in \argmin_{h\in\hyp} R(h)
\]
be the model that attains $R^*_\hyp$. Again, at least one such model exists since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty. By applying Bernstein inequality (see Theorem~\ref{app: bernstein} in the Appendix) to the variable $\hat R_D(h_0)$, we obtain a bound on $\hat R_D(\hat h_0)$ that depends on $\expv_D[\hat R_D(h_0)] = R^*_\hyp$. 

Then, using that \[\hat R_D(\hat h_D) \le \hat R_D(h_0)\] by definition of empirical risk minimizer, we obtain a bound on $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$ that can be applied to the right hand side in the previous theorem, yielding a bound on the gap that depends on the best risk. 

\begin{remark}
  The key ingredient of Bernstein inequality is that it uses the second moment of the random variable to bound higher order moments. That is, it is a concentration result in terms of the variance of the random variable. When $\ell$ is the zero-one loss, $\ell(h(X),Y)$ is a Bernoulli random variable with variance $R(h)(1-R(h))\le R(h)$. Therefore, $R^*_\hyp$ is a measure of the lowest possible variance of $\ell_h$ over $\hyp$. This is strictly connected to the noise in the data, as explained in~\cite{boucheron2005theory}.\ \mati{da controllare hehe}
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Bound with best risk]\label{coroll: bound bestrisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_{\hyp}\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\end{corollary}

Let the Bayes risk be
\[
R^* := \inf_{h:\X\to\Y} R(h)
\] 
where the infimum runs over a superset of $\hyp$. The best risk can be decomposed as 
\[
R^*_\hyp = \underbrace{R^*}_{\text{Bayes risk}} + \underbrace{R^*_\hyp - R^*}_{\text{Approximation error}} =: \sigma^2 + A_\hyp
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
\item the Bayes risk $\sigma^2$ measures the classification error due to the irreducible noise $\varepsilon$, as it is the minimal error that any function $h:\X\to\Y$ necessarily makes;
\item the approximation error $A_\hyp$ is the excess error we make given that we consider a restricted set of predictors, $\hyp$.
\end{itemize}
Note that if there is no noise, then $R^* = 0$ and the Bayes risk is attained by the data-generating function $h^*$. Moreover, if the model attaining $R^*$ lives in the hypotheses class, then $A_\hyp = 0$.

\begin{corollary}\label{coroll: bound noiseandappr}
With probability $1-\delta$,
\[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{(A_\hyp + \sigma^2)\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
\]
\end{corollary}

Tail integrating and using the counting Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}, we obtain the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Noise and approximation error bound]\label{thm: bound noiseandappr}
  Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
  \begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\expv_D\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le \min\Bigg\{ &4\sqrt{\frac{(A_{\hyp}+\sigma^2)\left(\log (4\card)+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\card)+1}{n}, \\
&4\sqrt{\frac{(A_{\hyp}+\sigma^2)\left((2n+2)\log 2+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{(2n+2)\log 2+1}{n} \Bigg\}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}


{\bf Experimental design}

As compared to Sections~\ref{sec: unionbound} and~\ref{sec: rademmassart} that used a Gaussian noise, we switch to a label-flip noise with flip probability $1-p$. Therefore, we sample from $\X$ as before and we generate the labels as 
\[
y \leftarrow \sgn(h^*(x))\cdot b
\]
where $h^*$ is as above and $b$ is a random variable taking value $1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability $1-p$. We use $p = 0.8$ and $p\in\{0.6,0.8,0.95\}$ to inspect multiple levels of noise.

Since $p\ge 0.5$, the Bayes risk is 
\[
R^* = 1-p
\]
$R^*_\hyp$ is computed exactly using dynamic programming. We produce labels using our sine function and the Bernoulli error and we cluster the intervals where labels have the same sign, obtaining $L$ contiguous blocks. We create a dp $k\times 2$ matrix that stores the minimum risk for each number of maximum splits $k$, up to $\min(L-1, 2^d)$ where $d$ is the maximum depth we are considering, when the last assigned label is $1$ or $-1$ (the columns). Then, for each contiguous block we dynamically update the dp matrix summing the minimum risk among all trees with at most $k$ splits. In the end, we select the minimum risk among the tree predicting $1$ or $-1$ in the very last block. 

The rest of the experimental design is as above.

\sqrt{A_{\hyp}+\sigma^2}+1  \right)\right\}.
\end{equation}

\section{Growth function}\label{sec: growthf}

In this section, we introduce a new measure of the complexity of the hypotheses class, that considers classification vectors. This tool allows to make the intuition on the saturation of the test error due to characteristics of the decision tree algorithm that we discussed in Section~\ref{sec: rademmassart} more explicit. 

\begin{definition}[Growth function]
The growth function of $\hyp$ is
\[
 \Pi_{n}(\hyp):=\sup_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X}
 \left|\left\{(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)):h\in\hyp\right\}\right|.
\]
\end{definition}

The following chain of inequalities extends Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}. 
Given the set of images of a function $h$, applying a loss function to it can at most decrease the number of vectors in the set. At the same time, the images of the functions in $\hyp$ are at most as many as the functions in $\hyp$, and always less than or equal to $2^n$, the number of all possible $n$-long sequences of $1$ and $-1$.

The following extends Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}.

\begin{lemma}[Counting inequalities with growth function]\label{lemma: growth}
Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
 1\le N_D(\hyp)\le\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\Pi_n(\hyp)  = N_n(\hyp) = \sup_{S\in{(\X\times\Y)}^n} N_S(\hyp).
\]
\end{lemma}

The relation between $N_D$ and $\Pi_n$ makes it immediate to obtain growth-function versions of the bounds in Sections~\ref{sec: rademmassart} and~\ref{sec: bestrisk} (substitute $N(D)\le\Pi_n$ into Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} and $N_{2n} = \Pi_{2n}$ into Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}). Substituting also $\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}$ yields bounds~(\ref{eq: rademmassart bound}) and (\ref{eq: noiseandappr bound}). The bounds are the same, but the intuition that they saturate because the number of distinct classification vectors is at most $2^n$ is made more explicit. In other words, when we evaluate the performance of an algorithm, the models in $\hyp$ that the algorithm can learn are equivalent if they give the same classification. This result takes this into account when measuring the richness of the hypotheses class $\hyp$.
