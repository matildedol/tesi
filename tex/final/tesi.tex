\subsection{Setting}

Given a sample space $\Omega$, let inputs and labels be generated by $X:\Omega \to \mathcal X = \{\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_N\}$ and $Y: \Omega \to \mathcal Y = \{-1, 1\}$. Let $p$ be the joint probability distribution of $(X,Y)$ on $\mathcal X\times\mathcal Y$, with
\[
 p_{j,y}=\Pr[X=\bar x_j,Y=y],\qquad
 p_{j,y}\ge0,\qquad
 \sum_{j=1}^N\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}p_{j,y}=1.
\]
Let $D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n$ be a dataset of $n$ i.i.d.\ copies of $(X,Y)$ drawn from $p$. We use the term {\it dataset\/} to refer to the {\it random variable\/} generating the data throughout this work. We denote by $S = {(x_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^n$ a realization of it. Given this data, we want to learn a classifier $h:\X\to\Y$ that given a new input $x\in\X$ classifies it correctly. 
Let $\hyp \subseteq \{h:\mathcal X \to \Y\}$ be a nonempty, finite set of functions where we look for such a classifier.
Given a loss function $\ell:\R \times \mathcal Y \to \mathbb R^+$, let the true and empirical risks be
\[
    R(h) = \mathbb E_D(\ell(h(X), Y)) = \sum_{\bar x\in\mathcal X} \sum_{\bar y \in\{-1,1\}} \ell(h(\bar x), \bar y) p(\bar x, \bar y).
\]
and
\[
    \hat R_D (h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(X_i), Y_i).
\]
Because we do not have access to $p$, we select the {\it empirical risk minimizer\/} (ERM), 
\begin{equation*}\label{eq: ERM}
    \hat h_D \in \argmin_{h\in \hyp} \hat R_D (h)
\end{equation*}
which, importantly, is a random variable of the data $D$. Note that at least one such $\hat h_D$ exists, since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty. 

\subsection{Approach}

We study bounds on the expcted gap between the test and train error of the ERM, \begin{equation} \label{eq: target}
\expv_D\left[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right]
\end{equation}
% Note that since $\hat h_D$ and $\hat R_D$ depend on the same dataset $D$, $\expv_D[\hat R_D(\hat h_D)]\neq \expv_D[R(\hat h_D)]$, i.e.\ the empirical risk is a biased estimate of the true risk. A proof of this fact is found in Appendix~\ref{app: add res} (Fact~\ref{app: fact bias}). % non è vero ehhehee la devo ancora fare

This approach allows to obtain bounds on the risk of the learnt model $\hat h_D$ instead of uniform bounds over the whole space of hypotheses, as the algorithm may not explore it~\cite{bousquet2002stability,nagarajan2019uniform}. Moreover, the training error usually goes to $0$ in our setting where the dataset is smaller than the input space, hence a bound on~(\ref{eq: target}) corresponds to a bound on the test error for a large enough depth. In light of this, we refer to~(\ref{eq: ERM}) as a bound on the gap and on the test error interchangeably throughout this work.
We detach from the usual literature also in that we express concentration results as bounds on the expected value of the target quantity via tail integration, rather than using results in high probability or bounding the probability that this quantity is large. In fact, results in expected value are more practical for the experiments and their interpretation is clearer as compared to high-probability results. 

We state our initial results in a general setting, for arbitrary hypotheses classes and loss functions, while we develop our main results in the specific case of binary decision trees under the zero-one loss. Specifically, 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the zero-one loss is $\ell(h(x), y) = \mathbf{1} (h(x)\neq y) = \frac{1 - h(x)y}{2}$;
    \item we consider a number of hypotheses classes $\hyp$, each denoted by $\hyp_d$ and corresponding to the set of binary decision trees $h:\X\to\Y$ with depth at most $d$ for $d>0$, letting  $d$ range over increasing values. 
\end{itemize}
This is also the setting in which experiments are carried out. We test our theoretical findings by comparing them with empirical evidence. In doing so, we use the DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm from the scikit-learn Python library. This differs from the empirical risk minimization algorithm that appears in~(\ref{eq: ERM}) in that it is a greedy algorithm, which not always selects the best possible model. Effects of this difference are discussed in Section~\ref{sec: concl}. 

Each one of Sections~\ref{sec: unionbound} to~\ref{sec: growthf} is organized as follows. First, we present theoretical results in the most generic case given the setting above. Then, we describe the experimental design and state the final asymptotic bound, which we then plot alongside an empirical estimate of~(\ref{eq: target}) to assess how well it captures the qualitative behavior of the test error curve. We conclude each section commenting on the results. Finally, in Section~\ref{sec: concl} we conclude and discuss limitations and potential developments of our findings.

\section{Union bound over $\hyp$}\label{sec: unionbound}
In this section, we present a fundamental result on the behavior of the gap between the expected and true risk of the ERM. First, we make the following assumption.

\begin{assumption}[Boundedness of loss function]\label{ass: bounded loss}
    Given a set $A\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\to\R^+$,
    $\exists M\in\R, M >0$ s.t.
    \[
    \ell (a) \le M \qquad \forall a\in A.
    \]
\end{assumption}

A bounded loss function is crucial for obtaining all the upper bounds on the gap discussed in this work. Nonetheless, we now give a sufficient condition that allows to circumnavigate boundedness.

\begin{assumption}[Lipschitzness of loss function and bound at zero]\label{ass: lipsch loss}
Given a set $A\subseteq\R$ and $\ell:A\to\R^+$, 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\exists C>0$ s.t.
        \[ \forall a,a^\prime\in A, \vert \ell(a)-\ell(a^\prime)\vert \le C\vert a-a^\prime\vert\]

        \item $\exists C^\prime >0$ s.t.
        \[ \vert \ell(0)\vert \le C^\prime
        \]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop: suff assump}
    Let $A = \{a\in\R : \vert a \vert \le B\text{ for some constant } B \in \R\}$. Then Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss} $\Rightarrow$ Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss}. 
\end{proposition}

Therefore, it is sufficient to assume Lipschitzness of the loss function to obtain boundedness, assuming bounded inputs.

On top of this,
let $T:\R\to[-1,1]$,
\begin{equation}
    T(a) = \begin{cases}
        -1 & a \le -1\\
        a & -1<a<1, \qquad\qquad a\in A\\
        1 & a\ge 1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
be a {\it truncation map\/}. 
For any hypotheses class $\hyp = \{h:\X\to\Y\}$, we can always define $\hyp_T=\{ h_T\coloneq T\circ h : \X\to[-1,1] \vert h\in\mathcal{H}\}$ and take $A = \{(h(x),y) : h\in\hyp_T, x\in\X, y\in\Y = \{1,-1\}\}$ which satisfies the assumption of bounded inputs required by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. 

Therefore, Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump} and the truncation technique determine that Lipschitzness of the loss function immediately implies boundedness. The former is easier to verify, as Example~\ref{ex: logistic} shows, and does not depend on the hypotheses set. This provides a framework for extending the results in this work to an infinte $\hyp$, as we discuss in Section~\ref{sec: concl}.   


\begin{example}[Logistic loss function]\label{ex: logistic}
    Let $\ell (a) = \log (1+e^{-a}), \,a \in \R$ be the logistic loss function. Note that in our setting the function takes as input the product $h(x)\cdot y$.
    We have
    \[
    \ell'(a) = -\frac{e^{-a}}{1+e^{-a}} = -\frac{1}{1+e^a}\quad \Rightarrow \quad \vert \ell'(a)\vert < 1 \qquad\forall a \in \R
    \]
    hence 
    \[
    C = \sup_{a\in\R} \vert \ell^\prime\vert \le 1.
    \]
    Also,
    \[
    \ell(0) = \log 2 = C^\prime.
    \]
\end{example}

\begin{fact}
  The zero-one loss satisfies Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss} with $M=1$. 
\end{fact}

We are ready to state our first result. 

\begin{theorem}\label{thm: unionbound} Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. Then
\[                
  0 \le \expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le 2M\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp \vert}{2n}}
\]
\end{theorem}

The logarithmic dependence on $\card$ arises directly from a union bound (Proposition~\ref{app: unionbound}) applied over the hypotheses class. We establish the exponential concentration of $\hat{R}_D(h)$ for a single, fixed hypothesis $h \in \hyp$ using Hoeffding's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffding}). However, the ERM $\hat{h}_D$ is a random variable dependent on the data $D$. To bound its deviation, we require the concentration event to hold simultaneously for all functions $h \in \hyp$. Taking the union bound introduces a factor of $\card$ into the probability tail. Finally, integrating this tail to bound the expected value yields the $\sqrt{\log \card}$ dependence. 

This result formally demonstrates that the empirical risk of the ERM systematically underestimates its true risk, with the generalization gap scaling as $\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log \card}{n}}\right)$. We next present the experimental design to evaluate this bound for binary decision trees and compare its asymptotic behavior with the empirical test error curve.

{\bf Experimental design}

We create the support $\mathcal X$ as a discretization over $N= 10,000$ numbers of the interval $[0,10]$. We sample from it with the uniform measure $p_\X(\bar x) = \sum_{\bar y\in \Y}p(\bar x, \bar y) = \frac{1}{N}$. 
The law to generate the labels is
\[
y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}(h^*(x) + \sigma\varepsilon)
\] 
where $h^*:\X\to\Y$, is the data-generating function, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, is a Gaussian noise, and $\sigma>0$. 
First, we generate the labels with a centered parabola, so that roughly half of them are $1$ and half $-1$ in the interval $[0,10]$. 
Since an algorithm could learn such a function with few datapoints (see preliminary experiments in Appendix~\ref{app/ sec: datagen}), we switch to a sinusoid with varying frequency,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: sine}
    h^*(x) = 5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right)
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the number of times $h^*$ crosses the $x$-axis in the open interval $(0,10)$, yielding $m+1$ contiguous blocks of $1s$ and $-1s$. 
We set as default value $m=10$.

We test a Bernoullian noise instead of a Gaussian one, concluding that there is no interesting difference in the performance of the algorithm as long as the portion of misclassified inputs due to the noise is the same (see preliminary experiments in Appendix~\ref{app/ sec: noise}). We find that fixing $m = 10$, $\sigma = 3.5$ achieves the same noise as $p =0.8$ (roughly 20\% of labels flipped). Thus, we stick to a Gaussian noise.

All in all, our labels are generated as
 \[
    y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}\left(5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right) + \sigma\varepsilon\right)
 \]
 with $\sigma = 3.5$ and $m = 10$. $\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)$ maps $0$ to $1$. 

We proceed as follows. We generate a test set with size $n_\text{test} = 200,000$ sampling from $\X$ with $p_\X$ and with replacement, and generating the labels as above. Then, for each depth $d \in [1, 7\lceil{\log_2 n}\rceil] \subset \mathbb N$ we do the following. For a number $T=100$ of iterations, we 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\cdot$] generate a train set ($n= 50$);
    \item[$\cdot$] fit a decision tree with depth at most $d$ (DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm from the scikit-learn library);
    \item[$\cdot$] compute the train error, the test error, and the differene between the two (the gap).
\end{itemize}
Then, we average the resulting quantities over the $T$ samples, obtaining empirical estimates of the expected values of the train error, test error, and gap over the disorder of the data. 

To plot the theoretical bound, instead, we use $\vert \hyp_d\vert = 2^d$.
% The exact computation gives
% \[
% \vert \hyp_d \vert = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\min{N-1, 2^d-1}} \binom{N-1}{k}
% \] 
% which is exponential in $d$. 

As the maximum depth $d$ increases, we plot the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[1.] the train and the test error curves;
    \item[2.] the gap and the theoretical bound;
    \item[3.] the test error and the sum of train error and the bound (moving $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$ to the right).
\end{enumerate}

Considering the zero-one loss, and ignoring constants since we are interested in the asymptotic behavior, Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq: union bound}
\expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_{D,d})] \le O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \hyp_d \vert}{n}}\right) \qquad \text{for }d>0.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{outputs/pic1.png}
  \caption{Union bound (Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}) over $\hyp_d$. Train and test error, gap against the bound, and test error against train error plus the bound, as functions of the maximum depth $d$; $n = 50$, $m = 10$, Gaussian noise with $\sigma = 3.5$.}
  \label{fig:pic1}
\end{figure}

We plot the bound~(\ref{eq: union bound}) in Figure~\ref{fig:pic1}. The first thing catching the eye is that the bound diverges, while the test error saturates. The divergence is due to the dependence of the bound on $\vert\hyp_d\vert$, that is the number of binary trees with maximum depth $d$, which clearly increases with $d$. It follows immediately that a complexity measure of the hypotheses set that naively counts the functions it contains does not satisfy our requirements to find a qualitatively informative bound.

The test error of binary decision trees saturates because of a specific characteristic of the algorithm. As soon as the learned tree has only ``pure'' leaves, i.e.\ leaves containing only inputs that have the same label in the training set, the algortihm ceases to split them and hence any additional allowed depth does not change the learned model. Clearly, the cardinality of the hypotheses sets $\vert\hyp_d\vert$ does not capture this property. 

Instead, a counting way that accounts for the finiteness of our problem might reproduce the saturation of the test error curve. Specifically, the configuration with only pure leaves can be achieved for two reasons: either all the possible values that the inputs can take are classified, or all the inputs in the sample are classified. Since in our case $n\ll N$, the second event happens much earlier than the first one. As a consequence, a complexity measure that counts the number of distinct classifications that the functions in $\hyp$ can give, i.e.\ the vectors $h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n) : h\in \hyp$, instead of the functions themselves, could more faithfully represent the true complexity of the space of models that the algorithm can practically learn and yield a bound that better captures the empirical test error curve.

\section{Using the Rademacher complexity}\label{sec: rademmassart}
In this section, we introduce the Rademacher complexity, a measure of expressiveness of a class of functions. This tool allows to leverage the viewpoint exposed in the last section and obtain a saturating bound on the test error of decision trees. As before, we state the first results for generic loss functions and hypotheses sets.  

\begin{definition}[Empirical Rademacher complexity]\label{def: rad gen}
Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ be $n$ i.i.d.\ random variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. Let $\mathcal{Z} \subset \R$ be a set and $\{z_1,\ldots,z_n\}\subset \mathcal{Z}$ a collection of $n$ points. Given $\mathcal G\coloneq \{g:\mathcal{Z} \to \R\}$ a class of functions over $\mathcal{Z}$, the Rademacher complexity of $\mathcal G$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\mathcal G) \coloneq \expv_{\xi} \left[\sup_{g \in \mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i g(z_i)\right]
\]
\end{definition}

The Rademacher complexity measures the richness of a class of functions. The argument of the supremum $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i\xi_i g(z_i)$ is the (scaled) inner product between $(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ and $(g(z_1),\ldots,g(z_n))$, measuring how well functions in $\mathcal{G}$ can align to the Rademacher variables.

In the context of our learning problem, we look at the Rademacher complexity of the class $\LL =  \{\ell_h:\X\times\Y\to\R^+ :\ell_h(x,y) = \ell(h(x),y), h \in \hyp \}$. 

\begin{definition}[Rademacher complexity of $\LL$]\label{def: rad}
Let $\xi = (\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ be independent random variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. The Rademacher complexity of the class $\LL$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \coloneq \expv_{\xi,D} \left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell_h(X_i, Y_i)\right]
\]
\end{definition}

Note that we also take the expectation over the disorder of the data $D$, because our sample $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n)$ is random and our quantity of interest~\ref{eq: ERM} is independent of the data realization. 
In this case, the argument of the supremum is the inner product between $(\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n)$ and $(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n))$. Since the loss function is fixed, it measures to what extent classifiers $h\in\hyp$ can align to random noise. Note that the supremum is taken before the expectation over the disorder of $\xi$ and $D$, and that it is a maximum, since $\LL$ is finite. Hence, for each dataset and for each realization of the Rademacher vectors, we can choose a different function $\ell_h \in\LL$ that attains the maximum. A high complexity means that for most values of $\xi$ we can find a model $h$ that is similar to that pattern. It is the highest when we can find a function that correlates with any possible pattern.

The following results hold. 

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher complexity bound]\label{thm: rad bound} [\cite{bach2024learning}, Proposition 4.2]
\[
\expv_D\left[\,\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\le 2 \mathcal R_n(\LL)
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Massart]\label{lemma: massart} [\cite{mohri2018foundations}, Theorem 3.3]
Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \vert\LL\vert}{n}}.
\]
\end{lemma}

Note that Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} can be easily extended to a bound on the gap at $\hat h_D$ because
\[
\expv_D[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)] \le \expv_D\left[\sup_{h \in \hyp}(R - \hat R_D)(h)\right]
\]
since $\hat h_D\in\hyp$. 

\begin{remark}
  %[Equivalence with Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}]
  We have $\vert\LL\vert \le \card$ by definition of $\LL$.
\end{remark}

Altogether, the previous results yield a bound on the gap at the ERM that is asymptotically the same as that provided by Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}. Now, we introduce some technology that allows to take a step forward. 

Let \[\LL(D) \coloneq \left\{\left(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)\right): h \in \hyp, D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n \right\}\] be the set of loss vectors for a random dataset $D$, i.e.\ the set containing the functions in $\LL$ applied to $D$. Let $v_i = \ell_h(X_i,Y_i)$ to ease notation. 

The supremum in Definition~\ref{def: rad} can be equivalently computed over the set of loss vectors,
\[
\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xi_i \ell_h(X_i,Y_i) = \sup_{(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\in\LL(D)} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xi_i v_i
\]

This yields the following version of the lemma. 

Let \[N_D(\hyp)\coloneq \left\vert\left\{\left(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)\right) : h\in\hyp, D = {((X_i, Y_i))}_{i=1}^n\right\}\right\vert = \left\vert \LL(D)\right\vert\] be the cardinality of the set of loss vectors for the hypotheses in $hyp$, which is a random variable as it depends on the dataset $D$.

\begin{lemma}[Massart, loss vectors]\label{lemma: massart loss v}
Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump}. Then
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \expv_D[N_D(\hyp)]}{n}}
\]
\end{lemma}

So, using the Rademacher complexity as an intermediate step allows us to replace the number of predictors $\card$ from Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound} with the expected number of loss vectors $N_D(\hyp)$ (up to constants). 
In fact, Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound} bounds a quantity that depends on the true expected value of $\ell_h(X,Y)$ with a quantity that can be computed on loss vectors. The key ingredient that allows this is a symmetrization technique, which consists in replacing the deviation of the random variable $\ell_h(X,Y)$ from its expected value with the difference between two random variables $\ell_h(X,Y)$ and $\ell_h(X',Y')$ taking two i.i.d.\ copies $D$ and $D'$ as inputs. Note that since the loss function is fixed, counting loss vectors is equivalent to counting classification vectors. We work with loss vectors here because they arise more naturally from the Rademacher complexity, and turn to classification vectors in Section~\ref{sec: growthf}. 

The following result encloses the key point of the saturation mechanism discussed at the end of Section~\ref{sec: unionbound}. 

\begin{lemma}[Counting]\label{lemma: counting} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
N_{D}(\hyp) \le \min\{\vert\hyp\vert, 2^n\} 
\]
surely.
\end{lemma} 

Substituting this bound on $N_D$ in Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} and combining it with Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}, we obtain the following bound. 

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher and Massart bound]\label{thm: rademmassart bound} Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
\expv_D[(R-\hat R_D) (\hat h_D)]\le \min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\card}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} 
\]
\end{theorem}

The experimental design is the same as above. Again, we ignore the constants and use
\begin{equation}\label{eq: rademmassart bound}
\min\left\{2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\hyp_d\vert}{n}}, 2\sqrt{2\log 2}\right\} = \min\left\{O\left(\sqrt\frac{\log\vert\hyp_d\vert}{n}\right), O(1)\right\}.
\end{equation}
We set the constant term to $0.5$, because the Rademacher complexity of $\LL$ is naturally bounded by $0.5$ (see Appendix~\ref{app: add res} for a proof of this fact).

We plot bound~(\ref{eq: rademmassart bound}) against the empirical error curve in Figure~\ref{fig:pic2}. For smaller depths, the bound is the same as in Figure~\ref{fig:pic1}. From a depth of $13$ onwards, the minimum is instead attained by the constant term, hence the bound saturates. This reflects the fact that beyond a certain depth, the number of distinct classification vectors no longer increases with $d$ through the mechanism explained above, hence the test error does not change. 

At the same time, this mechanism is myopic to the other aspects of the structure of the learning problem.
Indeed, Figure~\ref{fig:pic3} shows how the gap curve is sensitive to different levels of noise in the dataset, as measured by the $\sigma$ coefficient (standard deviation). We use $\sigma \in \{12.5, 3.5, 1\}$, which flip on average $40\%$, $20\%$ and $5\%$ of the labels. Specifically, it remains flatter with less noise, indicating both that the model performs better and that the difference between train and test error is lower (see Appendix~\ref{app: prel exp} for experiments showing how the empirical curves change with noise). The bound curve, instead, remains unchanged, failing to capture that the noise present in the data has an effect on the generalization performance of the algorithm.

\section{Dependence on the best risk}\label{sec: bestrisk}
In this section, we implement a different approach and new theoretical tools to look for a noise-sensitive bound.

Let \[N_S(\hyp) := \left\vert\{(\ell_h(x_1,y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(x_n,y_n):h\in\hyp, S = ((x_i,y_i))_{i=1}^n\}\right\vert\] be a realization of $N_D(\hyp)$ for a fixed dataset $S$, i.e.\ the number of all possible loss vectors for the hypotheses in $\hyp$ given $S$. Moreover, let \[N_{n}(\hyp) := \sup_{S \in {(\X \times \Y)}^n} N_S(\hyp)\] be the maximum possible cardinality of the set of loss vectors for the hypotheses in $\hyp$ over all datasets of size $n$. Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss for the rest of this section.

\begin{lemma}[Relative deviation]\label{lemma: rel dev}
  For $\varepsilon>0$ with $n\varepsilon^2 \ge 8$,
  \[
  \Pr\left[\exists h\in\hyp : (R-\hat R_D)(h) > \varepsilon\sqrt{R(h)}\right] \le 2N_{2n}(\hyp)e^{-n\varepsilon^2/16}.
  \]
\end{lemma}

The result above is a concentration result for the random variable $\hat R_D (h)$ in terms of the true risk $R(h)$. To obtain it, the key ingredient is Chebyshev inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: chebyshev}) that bounds the deviation of a random variable in terms of its variance. When a random variable is bounded, moreover, its variance is controlled by its expected risk because, intuitively, it cannot fluctuate much around its center. In particular, for $\ell_h(X,Y)$ that is bounded and Bernoulli, we have
\[
\Var(\ell_h) = \expv[\ell_h](1-\expv[\ell_h])\le \expv[\ell_h] = R(h).
\]
This explains how we get a bound on the deviation relative to the true risk. 
The bound we obtain, moreover, depends on $N_{2n}(\hyp)$, the maximum number of loss vectors for datasets of size $2n$. This quantity arises naturally because the result uses a symmetrization technique. Also, as presented in Section~\ref{sec: rademmassart}, this technique considers two independent datasets $D$ and $D'$ and we count loss vectors over their union, hence the size $2n$ instead of $n$. Note that this result can be obtained also in terms of $N_{D\cup D'}(\hyp)$, but to ease computations and avoid having to account for the randomness of the data $D$ we prefer taking the supremum.

In high probability, and isolating the empirical risk $\hat R_D$ on the right hand side, we obtain the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Bound with empirical risk]\label{thm: bound emprisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{\hat R_D(\hat h_D)\log(2N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 16\frac{\log(2N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\end{theorem}

We now want to bound the empirical risk of the ERM $\hat R_D(\hat h_D)$ in the right-hand side of the result above. 

Let
\[
R^*_\hyp = \min_{h\in\hyp} R(h)
\]
be the minimum risk attainable in the hypotheses set
and let \[
h_b \in \argmin_{h\in\hyp} R(h)
\]
be the best hypothesis, i.e.\ the one attaining $R^*_\hyp$. Again, at least one such model exists since $\hyp$ is finite and nonempty. 

We have
\begin{equation}\label{eq: propERM}
\hat R_D(\hat h_D) \le \hat R_D (h_b)
\end{equation}
surely, by definition of ERM. Moreover, Bernstein's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: bernstein}) applied to $\hat R_D(h_b)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq: bernstein at hb}
\hat R_D(h_b) \le R^*_{\hyp} + \sqrt{\frac{2R^*_\hyp \log (2/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{2\log(2/\delta)}{3n}.
\end{equation}
Note that the dependence on the best risk $R^*_\hyp$ appears because Bernstein's is a concentration result in terms of the variance, like Chebyshev's. As explained above, the variance of a bounded random variable like $\ell_{h_b}$ is $\Var(\ell_{h_b}) \le \expv[\ell_{h_b}] = R^*_\hyp$.

Therefore, inequalities~(\ref{eq: propERM}) and~(\ref{eq: bernstein at hb}) allow to bound $\hat R(\hat h_D)$ in terms of the best risk. Substituting this into Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk} yields a bound on the gap that depends on the best risk, as the following result shows. 

\begin{corollary}[Bound with best risk]\label{coroll: bound bestrisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{R^*_{\hyp}\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\end{corollary}

Finally, we make the dependence on the noise explicit. 

Let
\[
R^* := \inf_{h:\X\to\Y} R(h)
\] 
be the Bayes risk. Note that the infimum in the definition runs over a superset of $\hyp$, hence $R^* \le R^*_\hyp$. The best risk can be decomposed as 
\[
R^*_\hyp = \underbrace{R^*}_{\text{Bayes risk}} + \underbrace{R^*_\hyp - R^*}_{\text{Approximation error}} =: R^* + A_\hyp
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
\item the Bayes risk $R^*$ measures the classification error due to the irreducible noise $\varepsilon$, as it is the minimal error that any function $h:\X\to\Y$ necessarily makes;
\item the approximation error $A_\hyp$ is the excess error we make given that we consider a restricted set of predictors, $\hyp$.
\end{itemize}
Note that if there is no noise, then $R^* = 0$ and the Bayes risk is attained by the data-generating function $h^*$. Moreover, if the model attaining $R^*$ lives in the hypotheses class, then $A_\hyp = 0$.  

Substituting into Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk} we obtain the following result.

\begin{corollary}\label{coroll: bound noiseandappr}
With probability $1-\delta$,
\[
    (R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D) \le 4\sqrt{\frac{( R^*+A_\hyp)\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\hyp)/\delta)}{n}
\]
\end{corollary}

Tail integrating and using the counting lemma (Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}) we obtain the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Noise and approximation error bound]\label{thm: bound noiseandappr}
  Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
  \begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\expv_D\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le \min\Bigg\{ &4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\left(\log (4\card)+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\card)+1}{n}, \\
&4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_{\hyp})\left((2n+2)\log 2+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{(2n+2)\log 2+1}{n} \Bigg\}
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}

The result above is a bound on the expected gap at the ERM that explicitly depends on the noise $R^*$ while taking into account the finiteness of our problem via the counting lemma. 
% We have obtained it by using more advanced concentration inequalities that take into account the variance of the random variable, while Hoeffding's inequality and Hoeffding's lemma used in Section~\ref{sec: unionbound} and~\ref{sec: rademmassart} are myopic to the distribution of the random variable and only use that it ranges over a bounded interval. Also, the result is specific to the ERM $\hat h_D$, whose empirical risk can be bounded in terms of the best risk. The best risk achievable by our hypotheses, in turn, is at least equal to the risk due to irreducible noise in the data.
Next, we plot it and investigate how it relates to the empirical error curve. 

{\bf Experimental design}

As compared to Sections~\ref{sec: unionbound} and~\ref{sec: rademmassart} that used a Gaussian noise, we switch to a label-flip noise with flip probability $1-p$. This makes the computation of the Bayes risk and of the best risk easier. Therefore, we generate the labels as 
\[

\]
where $h^*$ is the sine function~(\ref{eq: sine}) and $b$ is a random variable taking value $1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability $1-p$. We use $p\in\{0.6,0.8,0.95\}$.

Since $p\ge 0.5$, the Bayes risk is

This is because the hypotheses attaining the Bayes risk selects the most probable label for each input often ({\it Bayes classifier\/} in Section 2 of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}). Moreover, we notice that for smaller depths the bound presents a non-linear and non-monotonic part, which is more evident with noise at $5\%$. We now aim at obtaining a better understanding of this curve.

\begin{enumerate}

    \item[(2)] for $d\ge d_c$, the best risk saturates and the saturating level is lower with less noise. 
\end{enumerate}
We suppose that the first dynamic is due to the fact that, with less noise, there are fewer contiguous block with the same label, therefore a marginal increase in the depth from $d$ to $d'$ (i.e., allowing $2^{d'}-2^d$ additional leaves) results in a larger decrease of the error, because, morally, more inputs will change classification in a single shot. Through these two mechanisms, the bound, seen as a function of the best risk, captures the dependence of the test error of the ERM on the noise. In particular, this explains the shape of the bound that we observed in Figure~\ref{fig:pic5}. The initial non-linearity is explained by mechanism $(1)$, while the upward shift of the bound curve as noise increases is motivated by $(2)$.

\section{Growth function}\label{sec: growthf}

In this section, we introduce the growth function, a new measure of complexity of the hypotheses class that considers classification vectors instead of loss vectors. This tool allows to make the intuition on the saturation of the test error due to characteristics of the decision tree algorithm that we discussed in Section~\ref{sec: rademmassart} more explicit. 

\begin{definition}[Growth function of $\hyp$]
The growth function of $\hyp$ is
\[
 \Pi_{n}(\hyp):=\sup_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X}
 \left|\left\{(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)):h\in\hyp\right\}\right|.
\]
\end{definition}

The following extends Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}.

\begin{lemma}[Counting with growth function]\label{lemma: growth}
Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
 1\le N_D(\hyp)\le\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}
\]
surely. Moreover,
\[
\Pi_n(\hyp)  = N_n(\hyp) = \sup_{S\in{(\X\times\Y)}^n} N_S(\hyp).
\]
\end{lemma}

Morally, applying $\ell$ to the image set of $h$ can at most decrease the number of vectors in the set, hence $N_D\le N_n = \Pi_n$. At the same time, the images of the functions in $\hyp$ are at most as many as the functions in $\hyp$, and always less than or equal to $2^n$, the number of all possible $n$-long sequences of $1$ and $-1$.

The relation between $N_D$ and $\Pi_n$ makes it immediate to obtain growth-function versions of the bounds in Sections~\ref{sec: rademmassart} and~\ref{sec: bestrisk} (substitute $N(D)\le\Pi_n$ into Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart loss v} and $N_{2n} = \Pi_{2n}$ into Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}). Substituting also $\Pi_{n}(\hyp)\le\min\{\vert \hyp \vert,2^n\}$ yields exactly bounds~(\ref{eq: rademmassart bound}) and (\ref{eq: noiseandappr bound}). The bounds are the same, but the intuition that they saturate because the number of distinct classification vectors is at most $2^n$ is made more explicit. In other words, when we evaluate the performance of an algorithm, the models in $\hyp$ that the algorithm can learn are equivalent if they give the same classification. This result takes this into account when measuring the richness of the hypotheses class $\hyp$. 

Therefore, the following Theorem constitutes our final result. 

\begin{theorem}[Bound with growth function]\label{thm: FINALE}
    Let $\ell$ be the zero-one loss. Then
\[
\expv_D\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le 4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*+A_\hyp)\left(\log (4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp))+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp))+1}{n}
\]
with
\[
\Pi_{2n}(\hyp) \le \min\left\{\vert\hyp\vert, 2^{2n}\right\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

This result provides an intuition on the drivers of the test error of decision trees. One one side, the bound it provides depends on the maximum number of classification vectors $\Pi_{2n}$, representing the maximum cardinality of the hypotheses class in practice. On the other side, the bound is a function of the noise $R^*$ and the approximation error $ A_{\hyp}$, capturing the effect that the noise in the data and our choice of hypotheses have on generalization. We empirically validate this with the experiments presented in Figure~\ref{fig:pic4} to~\ref{fig:pic7}. As such, the result provides a generalization bound that captures the behavior of the test error of decision trees as model complexity increases.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec: concl}

This work has inquired and empirically validated classical generalization bounds for decision trees. We have started from a bound using Hoeffing's inequality and a union bound over the hypotehses set, which did not saturate. Then, we have taken into account the fact that the class of functions that decision trees can learn is less complex than the one considered initially, due to the finite nature of the algorithm. By including this in our analysis via Lemma~\ref{lemma: growth}, we have obtained a saturating bound. Finally, 
we have noticed that the generalization performance of decision trees is affected by the level of noise in the data. Thus, we have obtained a bound that is a function of the lowest risk that a decision tree can attain, that depends on the noise through the Bayes risk. This, combined with the result on the actual complexity of the hypotheses space, has given a bound that saturates and that is sensitive to the noise. Theorem~\ref{thm: FINALE} summarizes this result. 

The main limitation of this work is given by the lack of an analysis of the difference between the selected model that we consider for theortical results, i.e.\ the Empirical Risk Minimizer, and the model learned in practie by the DecisionTreeClassifier algorithm. In fact, this is a greedy algorithm that not always selected the best posssible model. 

Apart from analyzing this criticality, the most promising direction for extending this thesis in the future is to apply the framework and approach proposed here to other algorithms. Specifically, we propose Random Fourier Features, Random Forests, and neural networks trained with Stochastic Gradient Descent as main developments.
