In machine learning, there exists a large number of algorithms, notably neural networks trained with gradient descent, which achieve great performance at prediction tasks on unseen data. 
The process of applying a model that has been learned on some available data to new, unseen data is called {\it generalization\/}.
A central question in learning theory is what drives the generalization ability of a learning algorithm~\cite{zhang2016understanding,neyshabur2014search}. Several works have addressed this question, suggesting that classical theory does not fully explain generalization. In particular, this means that it can fail to justify the behavior of the test error as the complexity of the hypothesis class increases~\cite{belkin2019reconciling,zhang2016understanding,zhang2021understanding,nagarajan2019uniform}.

This thesis contributes to this discussion by testing classical theoretical results against empirical evidence. Specifically, we study generalization bounds. A generalization bound is a bound on the {\it generalization gap}, i.e.\ the difference between the true and the empirical risk of a model selected on some available data $D$, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: target}
\expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right]
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken over the disorder of the data. Then, we inquire into the empirical validity of these bounds as functions capturing the {\it qualitative behavior\/} of the empirical generalization gap as model complexity increases. Unlike the traditional use of these results, we are not concerned with finding a quantitatively exact bound on the generalization gap, but one that reproduces its shape. To do so, we run experiments in the setting of a finite hypothesis class of binary decision trees under the zero-one loss. We look for a function that increasingly accounts for the structural aspects of our learning problem. The final result of this thesis is a bound on the generalization gap of decision trees that captures its saturating behavior and that is sensitive to the noise level in the data, summarized in the following theorem. 

\begin{theorem}
Let $R^*_\hyp = \min_{h\in\hyp} R(h)$ be the best risk attainable by hypotheses. Then
\[
\expv\left[(R-\hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right] \le 4\sqrt{\frac{(R^*_\hyp)\left(\log (4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp))+1\right)}{n}} + 20\,\frac{\log(4\Pi_{2n}(\hyp))+1}{n}
\]
with
\[
\Pi_{2n}(\hyp) \le \min\left\{\vert\hyp\vert, 2^{2n}\right\}.
\]
\end{theorem}

Hence, the main contribution of this work is to provide an empirical analysis of the limits and possibilities of classical bounds as descriptive tools. Hopefully, we also develop a framework and approach that can be extended to the study of more advanced generalization bounds, as well as other learning algorithms, like gradient-based ones.
