This section presents the state of the art and how this thesis contributes to it.

\medskip

\paragraph{On the empirical evaluation of generalization bounds}
Existing empirical studies of generalization bounds for decision trees mainly compare their numerical tightness~\cite{kaariainen2005comparison,pichuka2007tighter} or use them for pruning and model selection~\cite{kaariainen2004selective,leboeuf2022generalization,yildiz2015vc}. Our interest is different: we consider hypotheses sets $\hyp_d$ fixed in advance and compare the shape of analytical bounds with the empirical generalization gap as tree depth increases. Other works refine bounds through dependence on the training sample or an effective number of leaves~\cite{mansour2000generalization,golea1998generalization,bartlett2002rademacher}, without empirically evaluating their descriptive power. Outside decision trees,~\cite{jiang2019fantastic} investigate how complexity measures relate to generalization in neural networks. We share this interest in comparing theory with observed generalization, focusing on what classical bounds can explain for decision trees.

\medskip
\paragraph{Theoretical surveys of generalization bounds}
The survey~\cite{boucheron2005theory} has been a fundamental guide for the theoretical side of this thesis. Its emphasis, also shared by statistical analyses of CART~\cite{klusowski2024large}, is on how risk bounds behave as the sample size $n$ grows. We, instead, keep $n$ fixed and study bounds as functions of the complexity of the hypotheses set. This changes the role of the theoretical tools: our Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}, analogous to Theorem 5.1 of~\cite{boucheron2005theory}, allows us to account for noise, whereas the survey mainly uses it to improve the dependence on $n$ when the training error is small.

In light of the related literature, the contribution of this work is to investigate the limits and possibilities of classical generalization bounds as descriptive tools. We use a modern approach entailing empirical tests, compared to the traditional literature on generalization bounds; moreover, we take a different viewpoint that considers bounds as functions capturing the qualitative behavior of the curve for descriptive purposes, instead of risk guarantees or model selection.
