\section{Mathematical framework}\label{sec: math}

We observe $x\in \X = \{\bar x_1, \ldots, \bar x_N\}\subset \R$, and $y\in \Y = \{-1,1\}$ and we would like to predict $y$ from $x$.
To do so, we are given a collection of observations like $x$ and $y$, i.e.\ our data. This allows to observe the relationship between $x$ and $y$ multiple times and hope to estimate it well.
% am i biased by causal inference nel parlare di relazione tra x e y? alla fine dobbiamo sempre trovare una relazione, però è forse misleading dato che è occulta in (most of) machine learning 

\begin{assumption}\label{1ass: randomdata}
    We assume our data is drawn from a probability distribution.
Therefore, we consider the sample space $\Omega$ and define the random variables 
\[\begin{gathered}
    X:\Omega \to \X, \quad \omega \mapsto x \qquad \text{and}\qquad Y:\Omega \to \Y,\quad \eta \mapsto y;\\
    (X,Y)\sim p,
\end{gathered}\]
with joint law $p$, which is a cathegorical probability distribution over  $\X\times\Y = \{\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_N\}\times\{-1,1\}$.
\end{assumption}

Since we want to obtain a collection of observations, we define an additional random variable.

\begin{definition}[Dataset]
     Given a size $n$, a sample space $\Omega$, and value spaces $\X,\Y$, a dataset $D$ is a random variable 
    \[
    \begin{gathered}
        D:\Omega^n \to \X^n\times\Y^n, \qquad D\coloneq {(X_i, Y_i)}_{i=1}^n, \\
     (\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n)\mapsto ((x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n))\\
     D\sim p^n
        % D((\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n),(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n)):=((X_1(\omega_1),Y_1(\eta_1)),\ldots,(X_n(\omega_n),Y_n(\eta_n))),\quad D\sim p^n
    \end{gathered}
    \]
    where the $X_i$s and $Y_i$s are i.i.d copies of $X,Y$, and $X,Y,p$ are as in Assumption~\ref{1ass: randomdata}. We indicate with the lower-case $d$ realizations of $D$, $d={\{(x_i,y_i)\}}_{i=1}^n$. Also, we use $\mathcal{D}$ to indicate the output space of $D$ in general, and treat $\mathcal D = \X^n\times\Y^n$ as a particular case. 
\end{definition}

Now, predicting $y$ from $x$ means we want to find the best function $f$ such that $f(x)$ is a good approximation of $y$. To do so, we consider an {\it\/hypotheses space} $\cH$, which is the set of all functions $f:\X\to\Y$ that satisfy some hypotheses we choose to make on the relationship between $x$ and $y$.
\begin{fact}
    Functions in $\cH$ are deterministic objects.
\end{fact}

In order to find the best predictor $f$, we use a {\it\/learning algorithm}, in the idea of learning our predictor from the data. 

\begin{definition}[Learning algorithm]\label{1def: learningalg}
A learning algorithm $A$ is a deterministic function
\[
A:\mathcal D \to \mathcal H, \qquad d \mapsto \hat f_d
\]
that given some data $d\in \mathcal{D}$, outputs a prediction function $\hat f_d\in \mathcal{H}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    The hat notation $\hat f$ is used only to distinguish an element of $\cH$ that comes from a learning algorithm, or that depends on data, from a generic element of $\cH$, indicated as $f$. 
\end{remark}

Now, we are ready to define our central object, the predictor. 
\begin{definition}[Predictor]
    A predictor $\hat f_D$ is a random variable 
    \[
    \hat f_D : \Omega^n  \to \mathcal H, \qquad \hat f_D \coloneq A \circ D, \qquad {(\omega_i)}_{i=1}^n\mapsto d \mapsto \hat f_d
    \]
    that depends on some data $D$ and on a learning algorithm $A$. The source of randomness is the dataset $D$.  
\end{definition}

Now, we would like to have a way to {\it\/ evaluate} our predictors, in order to select the best one. To do so, we use a metric called {\it\/expected risk}. First, we take a loss function $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R^+$ that takes the preidction $f(x)$ and the true value $y$ and outputs a number corresponding to the error in the prediction. 

\begin{definition}[Expected risk]
     Given a predictor $f$ and a loss function $\ell$, the expected risk is a deterministic function $R: (\X\times\Y) \times \cH \to \R$,
    \begin{equation}
        R(f) \coloneq \sum_{\bar x \in \X} \sum_{y\in\{-1,1\}}\ell(f(\bar x),y)p(\bar x, y).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
The issue with using $R(f)$ as a metric, however, is that we do not know the true distribution $p$ in general. Hence, we approximate the expected risk with a new, emprical metric, the {\it\/empirical risk}, motivated by the law of large numbers. 

\begin{definition}[Empirical risk]
Given a dataset $D$, a predictor $f$, and a loss function $\ell$, the empirical risk is
    \begin{equation}
        \hat R_D(f) \coloneq \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(X_i),Y_i), \qquad D = (X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n).
    \end{equation}
    It is a random variable due to the randomness in $D$, in the sense that $\hat R_D \coloneq \hat R \circ D$ for a deterministic function $\hat R:\cH \times \mathcal{D}\to\R$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
    Notice that we implicitly define the two metrics as operators applying the loss function $\ell$ to their inputs, but we could equivalently define them taking inputs from $\R$, the image space of $\ell$. 
\end{remark}
For a deterministic estimator $f\in\cH$, the following result holds.

\begin{proposition}[Unbiasedness of the empirical risk]\label{1prop: unbiased}
    Given a scalar $a \in \R$, the empirical risk of $a$ is an unbiased estimator of the expected risk,
    \begin{equation}
    \expv[\hat R_D(a)] = R(a).
\end{equation}
\begin{proof}
A proof is found in the appendix (Proposition~\ref{app: unbiased}).
\end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\section{Statistical bounds on the risk}\label{sec: bias}

With the technology defined above, we define an estimator given a dataset $D$ and an hypotheses space $\cH$ as
\begin{equation}\label{1eq: argmin}
    \hat f_D \coloneq \argmin_{f\in\cH} \hat R_D(f).
\end{equation}
This means that among all candidates in $\cH$, we select the one that minimizes the empirical risk. Solving problem (\ref{1eq: argmin}) corresponds to {\it\/fitting} the estimator to the data $D$ or {\it\/training} the estimator on $D$. 

Subsequently, it is natural to look for a measure of performance of $\hat f_D$, for instance to choose the best one among a set of different hypotheses (e.g.\ different architectures of a neural network), or to provide a measure of reliability of our prediction. However, our estimator is now a random variable of the data. This makes the result in Proposition~\ref{1prop: unbiased} not valid anymore: the empirical risk computed on a dataset $D$ is a biased estimate of the true, expected risk of an estimator trained on $D$. This intuitively comes from the correlation between datapoints, as seen in Example~\ref{app: toy}. 
As a solution, classical statistical theory provides splitting the dataset into two parts $(D_{train},D_{test})=D$ to preserve unbiasedness. Usually, we fit the estimator to $D_{train}$ and compute $\hat R$ on $D_{test}$, to obtain $\hat R_{D_{test}}$ as an unbiased estimate of the risk of $\hat f_{D_{train}}$. 

We begin by asking the following question: how many splits of the dataset are really necessary in practice? To answer this, we want to study the bias arising from using the same dataset for training and evaluating a model. 

We make the following assumptions.

\begin{assumption}[Boundedness of loss function]\label{ass: bounded loss}
    Given a loss function $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R^+$ and a prediction function $f:\X\to\Y$, 
    $\exists M\in\R$ s.t.
    \[
    \ell (f(x), y) \le M \qquad \forall x\in\X, y\in\Y.
    \]
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Lipschitzness of loss function and bound at zero]\label{ass: lipsch loss}
Given a loss function $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R^+$ and a prediction function $f:\X\to\Y$, 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\exists C>0$ s.t.
        \[ \forall a,a^\prime\in\R, \vert \ell(a)-\ell(a^\prime)\vert \le C\vert a-a^\prime\vert\]

        \item $\exists C^\prime >0$ s.t.
        \[ \vert \ell(0)\vert \le C^\prime
        \]
    \end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Let us make an example of a loss function that satisfies these assumptions.

\begin{example}[Logistic loss function]\label{ex: logistic}
    Let $\ell (a) = \log (1+e^{-a}), a \in \R$. Notice that to apply this to our setting we take the product $f(x)\cdot y$ and then apply $\ell$.
    We have
    \[
    \ell'(a) = -\frac{e^{-a}}{1+e^{-a}} = -\frac{1}{1+e^a}\quad \Rightarrow \quad \vert \ell'(a)\vert < 1 \qquad\forall a \in \R
    \]
    hence 
    \[
    C = \sup_{a\in\R} \vert \ell^\prime\vert \le 1.
    \]
    Also,
    \[
    \ell(0) = \log 2 = C^\prime.
    \]
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop: suff assump 2.2}
    For $a\in A\subset\R, A\coloneqq \{a\in\R : \vert a \vert \le B\text{ for some constant } B \in \R\}$, Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss} $\Rightarrow$ Assumption~\ref{ass: bounded loss}. 
    \begin{proof} For $a\in A$,
        \begin{align*}
            \ell(a) &= \vert \ell(a)\vert \\
            &= \vert \ell(a)-\ell(0)+\ell(0)\vert \\
            &\le \vert \ell(a)-\ell(0)\vert + \vert \ell(0)\vert \\
            &\le C\vert a \vert + C^\prime \\
            &\le CB + C^\prime \eqqcolon M
        \end{align*}
    \end{proof} 
\end{proposition}
Notice that we can always take a {\it\/truncation map} $T:\R\to[-1,1]$, defined as 
\begin{equation}
    T(a) = \begin{cases}
        -1 & a \le -1\\
        a & -1<a<1\\
        1 & a\ge 1,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
so that $T\circ f(x)$ is bounded for any $f$ and $x$. Hence, we consider $A = \{T\circ f(x) : f \in\cH, x\in\X\}$ and $\mathcal{F}\coloneq\{f \vert f:\X \to \R\}$, $\cH \subseteq \mathcal{F}, \cH = \{T\circ f \vert f \in \mathcal{F}\}$. Now, we are ready to state our main result. 

\begin{theorem}[Bounds on the bias]\label{thm: main bound}
Consider data $D: \Omega^n \to \X^n \times \Y^n$, a predictor $\hat f_D \in \cH$, and a loss function $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\R^+$. Let $\ell$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ for some $M\in\R$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. Then, the empirical and the expected risk of $\hat f_D$ satisfy the following bound
\begin{empheq}[box=\fcolorbox{black}{white}]{equation}\label{eq: mainbounds}
    0 \le \expv_D[ R(\hat f_D) - \hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le 2M\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \cH \vert}{2n}}.
\end{empheq}
\begin{proof}
We prove the lower bound ($0\le\expv_D\ldots$) first. All the expectations are over the randomness of the data $D$, we drop the subscript to light notation. We have
\begin{equation}\label{ineq: minimizer1}
\hat R_D(\hat f_D) \le\hat R_D(f) \quad \forall f\in \cH,
\end{equation}
since $\hat f_D \coloneq \argmin_{f\in\cH} \hat R_D (f) $. 
Then, take expectations 
\begin{equation}
    \expv[\hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le \expv[\hat R_D(f)] \quad \forall f\in \cH.
\end{equation}
Now, since the functions in $\cH$ are deterministic, by Proposition~\ref{1prop: unbiased} we have
\begin{equation}
    \expv[\hat R_D(f)] = R(f) \quad \forall f\in\cH,
\end{equation}
so that 
\begin{equation}\label{1eq: keybound}
    \expv[\hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le R(f) \quad \forall f\in \cH.
\end{equation}
Now take $d$ a generic realization of $D$ and consider in particular $f=\hat f_d$,
\begin{equation}
    \expv[\hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le R(\hat f_d) \quad \forall d\in \mathcal D.
\end{equation}
Since this is valid pointwise for any $d$ realization of $D$, we have
\begin{equation}
    \expv[\hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le R(\hat f_D) \quad \text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
and, finally, taking expectations
\begin{equation}
        \expv[\hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le \expv[R(\hat f_D)] \quad \text{a.s.}
\end{equation}
Next, we prove the upper bound ($\expv \le 2 \sqrt{}\ldots$). For any $f \in \cH$, $\expv [\hat R_D (f)]=R(f)$. Let $Z_i \coloneq \ell(f(x_i),y_i)$ and have
\[
\hat R_D(f) =\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i 
\]
We now want to apply Hoeffding's inequality (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffding} in the appendix). To do so, we need to satisfy 3 conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $Z_1, \ldots, Z_n$ i.i.d.;
    \item $\expv[Z_1] = \cdots = \expv[Z_n]$;
    \item $a_i \le Z_i \le b_i$ for some $a_1, \ldots, a_n \in \R$ and $b_1,\ldots,b_n\in\R$. 
\end{enumerate}

We have that $(i)$ is satisfied by ${Z_i}s$ being i.i.d.\ by construction. Condition $(ii)$ is satisfied since all the $(x_i,y_i)$ have the same distribution. $(iii)$ is satisfied with $a_1=\cdots=a_n=0$ and $b_1 = \cdots = b_n = M$ by non-negativity of the loss function and Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2} with our definition of $\cH$. 
Now we can apply Hoeffding's. We obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    \Pr [\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Z_i - \mu \ge \varepsilon] \le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}
\end{equation*}
and thus
\begin{equation}\label{ineq: highprobbound}
    \Pr [ R(f) - \hat R_D(f) \ge \varepsilon]\le e^{- 2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}
\end{equation}
Now, to obtain a bound for $\hat f_D$ we bound it with the union of the probabilities of the events for each $f\in\cH$. 
We have
\begin{equation}
    \Pr[R(\hat f_D)-\hat R_D(\hat f_D) \ge \varepsilon] \le \bigcup_{f \in \cH} \Pr[R(f)-\hat R_D(f) \ge \varepsilon]
\end{equation}
trivially, and also 
\begin{equation}
    \bigcup_{f \in \cH} \Pr[R(f)-\hat R_D(f) \ge \varepsilon] \le \sum_{f \in \cH} \Pr[R(f)-\hat R_D(f) \ge \varepsilon] \le \sum_{f \in \cH} e^{- 2n\varepsilon^2/M^2} = \vert \cH \vert  e^{- 2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}
\end{equation}
where the first inequality follows by a union bound and the second by Hoeffding. All in all, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq: upper bound}
    \Pr[R(\hat f_D)-\hat R_D(\hat f_D) \ge \varepsilon] \le \vert \cH \vert  e^{- 2n\varepsilon^2/M^2}
\end{equation}

To obtain our initial inequality from this result on a probability, we use a technique called tail integration. The key ingredient is the following
\begin{equation}\label{eq: keying}
    \expv[X] = \int_0^\infty \Pr(X\ge \varepsilon) \de \varepsilon
\end{equation}
which is true for any non-negative random variable $X$, see Proposition~\ref{app: expvcmf} in the appendix. Then, we want to use our bound (\ref{eq: upper bound}) for the integrand. Let $\alpha\coloneq \vert\cH\vert$ and $c\coloneq 2n/M^2$, so that
\[
\Pr (X\ge \varepsilon) \le \alpha e^{-c\varepsilon^2}.
\] 
Notice that for high values of $\varepsilon$, the bound is a very good one, but for $\varepsilon \gg 1$ it is bad. Indeed, we can use a better bound:
\begin{equation}
    \Pr[X\ge \varepsilon] \le \min\{1, \alpha  e^{-c\varepsilon^2}\}
\end{equation}
Therefore, it makes sense to split the integral in (\ref{eq: keying}) in two parts with a threshold $\alpha  e^{-c\varepsilon_0^2} = 1$. This means splitting the integral at $\varepsilon_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\log \alpha}{c}}$. We obtain 
\begin{align*}
\expv[X] &= \int_0^\infty \Pr(X\ge \varepsilon) \de \varepsilon \le \int_0^{\sqrt\frac{\log \alpha}{c}} 1 \de \varepsilon + \int_{\sqrt\frac{\log \alpha}{c}}^\infty \alpha e^{-c\varepsilon^2}\de \varepsilon\\
&=\sqrt\frac{\log \alpha}{c} + \alpha\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c\log \alpha}}e^{-c\varepsilon^2} = \sqrt\frac{\log \alpha}{c} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{c\log \alpha}}
\end{align*}
where the second integral is solved with a standard Gaussian tail bound (see Proposition~\ref{app: gtailbound}), and in the last equality we substituted $\alpha = 1/e^{-c\varepsilon^2}$. Finally, we can use a looser but simpler bound assuming  $\alpha\ge e^{1/2}$, which is sensible since $\alpha=\vert\cH\vert $ and $e^{1/2}<2$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq}
    \expv[X] \le 2\sqrt\frac{\log \alpha}{c}
\end{equation}
which yields the result for $X = R(\hat f_D)-\hat R(\hat f_D)$, $\alpha = \vert \cH\vert$, and $c=2n/M^2$. 
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}[Bound for the logistic loss function]
Let us see how this bound looks like for a logistic loss function. By our definition of $\cH$ via the truncation map, we have $B=1$; then, $C = 1$ and $C^\prime = \log2$ by Example~\ref{ex: logistic}. Hence,
\begin{equation}
        0 \le \expv_D[ R(\hat f_D) - \hat R_D(\hat f_D)] \le (2+2\log 2)\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \cH \vert}{2n}}.
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\subsection{Rademacher Complexity and Symmetrization Trick}

In this section, we present a different bound from the one in Theorem~\ref{thm: main bound}, based on the so-called {\it\/Rademacher complexity} and on a {\it\/symmetrization trick}.
% We follow~\cite{bach2024learning}.

\begin{definition}[Rademacher complexity]\label{def: rademacher}
    Given a data size $n$, a dataset $D = {(X_i, Y_i)}_{i=1}^n$, a loss function $\ell:\Y\to\Y$, and an hypotheses space $\cH=\{f \vert f:\X\to\Y\}$, let $\xi$ be a $n$-dimensional vector of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables (i.e.\ taking values $1$ and $-1$ with equal probability). The Rademacher complexity measure $\mathfrak R_n$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \mathfrak R_n(\cH) \coloneq \expv_{\xi,D} [\sup_{f\in\cH} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell(Y_i, f(X_i))] = \expv_{\xi,D} \sup_{f\in\cH} \langle \xi, \ell(f(X),Y)\rangle
    \end{equation}
    and it is a deterministic object depending on $n, \cH$, and the distributions of $\xi$ and $D$. 
\end{definition}

% \begin{remark}
%     In our setting, where $\mathrm{Im}(f) = \{-1,1\}$, using $\mathbf{1}_{y_i f(x_i)}$ or directly $y_i f(x_i)$ is equivalent. 
% \end{remark}
Notice that to be precise we should call this quantity $\mathfrak{R}_n(\ell\circ\cH)$ (for instance following~\cite{boucheron2005theory}), but once we fix a loss function $\ell$, our quantity depends only on the elements of $\cH$ and indeed also the supremum runs over $\cH$. 


\begin{remark}
    The Rademacher complexity measures the highest possible alignment between the function $(x,y)\mapsto \ell(f(x),y)$ and the Rademacher random variable $\xi$. Since the loss function is given, it is a measure of the ``flexibility'' of $\cH$, hence its complexity.
\end{remark}

The Rademacher complexity controls the maximal deviation of the empirical risk $\hat R(f)$ from the true risk $R(f)$ through a symmetrization property (this is Proposition $4.2$ in~\cite{bach2024learning}). 

\begin{proposition}[Symmetrization]\label{prop: symmetriz}
    Consider data $D: \Omega^n \to \X^n \times \Y^n$ and an hypotheses set $\cH$. Given the Rademacher complexity $\mathfrak R_n$ as in Definition~\ref{def: rademacher}, the empirical and the expected risk of the estimators $f\in\cH$ satisfy the following bound
    \begin{equation}
        \expv_D[\sup_{f\in\cH} (R-\hat R_D) (f)] \le 2 \mathfrak R_n(\cH).
    \end{equation}
    \begin{proof}
        Let $h:(x,y)\mapsto(\ell(f(x),y))$ living in $\ell \circ \mathcal H$ and $Z\coloneqq(X,Y)$, so that
        \begin{equation*}
            R(f) =  \expv_D[h(Z)] \qquad \text{and} \qquad
            \hat R_D(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} h(Z_i).
        \end{equation*}
        The key ingredient of the proof is to introduce an independent copy of our dataset $D$, $D'$, and study the random variable $h(Z') - h(Z)$, which has a symmetric distribution and hence is easier to analyze ({\it\/ symmetrization trick}). 
        We proceed as follows. 
        Introduce $D^\prime = {(Z_i')}_{i=1}^n$ identical copies of $Z'$ and rewrite
        \begin{equation}
            \expv_D[h(Z)] = \expv_{D^\prime\vert D} [h(Z_i')\vert D].
        \end{equation}
        Then,
        \begin{align}
            \expv_D[\sup_{f\in\cH} (R-\hat R) (f)] &= \expv_D\bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \expv_D[h(Z)] - \frac{1}{n}\sum_i h(Z_i)\bigg] \notag\\
            &= \expv_D\bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \expv_{D^\prime\vert D} \Big[h(Z_i')\Big\vert D\Big] - \frac{1}{n}\sum_i h(Z_i)\bigg] \notag\\\notag
            &= \expv_D\bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \expv_{D^\prime\vert D} \Big[h(Z_i')\Big\vert D\Big]-  h(Z_i) \bigg]\\
            &= \expv_D\bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)}  \expv_{D^\prime\vert D} \Big[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_i h(Z_i')-  h(Z_i)\Big\vert D\Big] \bigg].
        \end{align} 
        Using that $f(X)\le \sup f(X) \Rightarrow \expv[f(X)]\le \expv[\sup f(X)] \Rightarrow \sup \expv[f(X)]\le \expv[\sup f(X)]$ and the law of total expectations, we obtain
        \begin{align}
            \expv_D[\sup_{f\in\cH} (R-\hat R) (f)] &\le \expv_D\bigg[ \expv_{D^\prime\vert D}\Big[ \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i h(Z_i')-  h(Z_i) \Big\vert D\Big]\bigg] \notag\\
            &=\expv_{D,D^\prime}\bigg[  \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i h(Z_i')-  h(Z_i) \bigg].\label{eq: 26}
        \end{align}
        Now we want to apply the symmetrization trick. 
        % parliamo direttamente di joint symmetry perché è questa che ci serve per concludere la cosa sul sup
        % la cit per questa parte è chat :((
        % quetsa proof si estende facilmente al caso H infinito perche si dimostra uguaglianza delle leggi per ogni collezione finita h1,...,hm e quindi si puo concludere (up to measurability or something) uguaglianza in legge dei due processi indexed by h\in\cH
        Let $m \coloneqq \vert\cH\vert$. For $i=1,\ldots,n$, the random variables $(h_1({Z_i}^\prime)-h_1(Z_i),\ldots, h_m({Z_i}^\prime)-h_m(Z_i))$ are jointly symmetric. 
        This is because $Z_i$ and ${Z_i}^\prime$ are identically distributed, hence 
        \begin{equation}
            {\big(h_j({Z_i}^\prime) -h_j(Z_i)\big)}_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} {\big(h_j(Z_i) - h_j({Z_i^\prime})\big)}_{j=1}^m.
        \end{equation}
        This means that for $e_i\in\{-1,1\}$,
        \begin{equation}
            {\big(h_j({Z_i}^\prime) -h_j(Z_i)\big)}_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} {\big(e_i (h_j({Z_i}^\prime) - h_j(Z_i))\big)}_{j=1}^m.
            %\qquad \forall i=1,\ldots,n, \forall h\in\ell\circ\cH
        \end{equation}
        Using independence over the $i$s we have 
        \begin{equation}
             {\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i h_j({Z_i}^\prime) -h_j(Z_i)\bigg)}_{j=1}^m \overset{d}{=} {\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i e_i(h_j({Z_i}^\prime) -h_j(Z_i))\bigg)}_{j=1}^m,
        \end{equation}
        and thus 
        \begin{equation}
            \sup_{h \in(\ell \circ\cH)} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i h({Z_i}^\prime) -h(Z_i) \overset{d}{=}\sup_{h \in(\ell\circ\cH)} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i e_i(h({Z_i}^\prime) -h(Z_i)),
        \end{equation}
        where since the supremum is a functional of the stochastic process, equality in distribution of the supremum follows from equality in distribution of the process.
        Hence, we obtain
        \begin{align}
            \expv_{D,D^\prime} \bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)}\frac{1}{n} \sum_i h({Z_i}^\prime)-h(Z_i)\bigg] &= \expv_{D,D^\prime} \bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_i e_i(h({Z_i}^\prime)-h(Z_i))\bigg]\\
            &= \expv_{D,D^\prime\vert\xi} \bigg[\sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xi_i(h({Z_i}^\prime)-h(Z_i))\big\vert \xi = e\bigg]\\
            &= \expv_{D,D^\prime,\xi}\bigg[  \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \xi_i(h(Z_i')-  h(Z_i)) \bigg].
        \end{align}
        where in the last row we take the expected value over the disorder of $\xi$ and use the tower law.
        Plugging this into inequality (\ref{eq: 26}), we obtain
        \begin{align*}
            \expv_D[\sup_{f\in\cH} (R-\hat R) (f)] &\le \expv_{D,D^\prime,\xi}\bigg[  \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \xi_i(h(Z_i')-  h(Z_i)) \bigg]\\
            &\le \expv_{D^\prime,\xi}\bigg[  \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \xi_i h(Z_i') \bigg] + \expv_{D,\xi}\bigg[ \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i - \xi_i h(Z_i) \bigg]\\
            &= \expv_{D^\prime,\xi}\bigg[  \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \xi_i h(Z_i') \bigg] + \expv_{D,\xi}\bigg[ \sup_{h\in(\ell\circ\mathcal H)} \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \xi_i h(Z_i) \bigg]\\
            &= 2 \mathfrak R_n(\cH)
        \end{align*}
        because $\xi h(Z) \overset{d}{=} -\xi h(Z)$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

The following result gives us an explicit bound on the Rademacher complexity. This is Theorem $3.3$ in~\cite{mohri2018foundations}. 

\begin{lemma}[Massart's lemma]\label{lemma: massart's lemma}
    Let $\ell:\Y\to\Y$ be as in Assumption~\ref{ass: lipsch loss}, so that $\ell(\cdot) \le M$ by Proposition~\ref{prop: suff assump 2.2}. Then, the Rademacher complexity of $\cH$ satisfies

    \begin{equation}
        \mathfrak R_n(\cH) = \expv_{\xi,D} \bigg[\sup_{f\in\cH} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i\ell(Y_i, f(X_i))\bigg] \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log \vert \cH \vert}{n}}.
    \end{equation}

    \begin{proof}
        For any $t>0$,
        \begin{align}
            \exp\bigg(t\, \expv_{\xi,D} \Big[\sup_{f\in\cH}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big]\bigg)
            &\le \expv_{\xi,D}\bigg[\exp\Big(t\sup_{f\in\cH}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big)\bigg]\notag\\
            &= \expv_{\xi,D}\bigg[\sup_{f\in\cH}\exp\Big(t\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big)\bigg]\notag\\
            &\le \expv_{\xi,D}\bigg[\sum_{f\in\cH}\exp\Big(t\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big)\bigg]\notag\\
            &= \sum_{f\in\cH}\prod_{i=1}^n \expv_{\xi_i}\Big[\exp\big(t\xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\big)\Big].\label{eq: massart-start}
        \end{align}
        where we use Jensen's inequality on the convex function $\exp$ in the first inequality, we trivially bound the supremum with the sum, and we use the independence of the $\xi_i$'s across $i=1,\ldots,n$ in the last equality. 

        Now, we want to apply Hoeffding's lemma (Theorem~\ref{app: hoeffdinglemma} in the appendix) to each factor. Notice that we can do so because each $\xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))$ is a centered random variable, since $\expv[\xi_i]=0$ and $\xi_i$ is independent of $\ell(Y_i,f(X_i))$, and it satisfies $\vert \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i)) \vert \le M$ by assumption. We obtain
        \begin{equation}
            \expv_{\xi_i}\Big[\exp\big(t\xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\big)\Big] \le e^{t^2M^2/2}.
        \end{equation}
        Plugging this back into (\ref{eq: massart-start}), we obtain
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: massart-hoeffding}
            \exp\bigg(t\, \expv_{\xi,D} \Big[\sup_{f\in\cH}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big]\bigg) \le \sum_{f\in\cH}\prod_{i=1}^n e^{t^2M^2/2} = \vert \cH \vert e^{nt^2M^2/2},
        \end{equation}
        Now take the log of both sides
        \begin{equation}
            t\, \expv_{\xi,D} \Big[\sup_{f\in\cH}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(Y_i,f(X_i))\Big] \le \log \vert \cH \vert + \frac{nt^2M^2}{2}.
        \end{equation}
        and divide  by $tn>0$, obtaining
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: massart-opt}
            \mathfrak R_n(\cH) \le \frac{\log\vert \cH\vert}{tn} + \frac{tM^2}{2}.
        \end{equation}
        Since (\ref{eq: massart-opt}) holds for every $t>0$, we look for the tightest bound. We have
        \begin{equation}
            -\frac{\log\vert\cH\vert}{t^2n} + \frac{M^2}{2} = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad t^\star = \sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\cH\vert}{nM^2}},
        \end{equation}
        and
        \begin{equation}
            2\frac{\log\vert\cH\vert}{t^3 n} \ge 0
        \end{equation}
        hence $t^*$ is a minimizer. Plugging it back into (\ref{eq: massart-opt}) yields
        \begin{equation}
            \mathfrak R_n(\cH) \le M\sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\cH\vert}{n}}.
        \end{equation}
  
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

Hence, putting together Proposition~\ref{prop: symmetriz} and Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart's lemma}, we obtain the following bound on the deviation of the empirical risk from the true risk
\begin{empheq}[box=\fcolorbox{black}{white}]{equation}\label{bound: rademacher}
    \expv_D[\sup_{f\in\cH} (R-\hat R_D) (f)] \le 2 M\sqrt{\frac{2\log\vert\cH\vert}{n}}.
\end{empheq}

\subsection{Noise analysis}

We report the results given by section 5.3 of~\cite{boucheron2005theory} and specify them to our setting.
\\

Let $\ell:\Y\times\Y\to\{0,1\}$ be the counting loss, $\ell(f(X),Y) = \mathbf{1}_{\{f(X)\neq Y\}}$ and consider a new set of functions $\mathcal G \coloneq \left\{ \mathbf{1}_{\{f(X)\neq Y\}} - \mathbf{1}_{\{f_\mathrm{opt}(X)\neq Y\}}\vert f\in\cH\right\}$ where $f_\mathrm{opt} \in \argmin_{f\in\cH} R(f)$ (this is indicated by $\mathcal F$ in the paper). We will indicate the elements of $\mathcal G$ as  $g_f$ for each $f\in\cH$. Then, $\expv[g_f] = \expv[\ell(f)] - \expv[\ell(f_\mathrm{opt})] = R(f) - R(f_\mathrm{opt})$. 

\begin{theorem}[Bound with noise]
    Consider the setting above. Assuming that 
    \begin{equation}
        \Var(f - f_\mathrm{opt}) \le {\left(\frac{R(f)-R(f_\mathrm{opt})}{h}\right)}^\alpha
    \end{equation}
    for some $h>0$, $\alpha \in (0,1]$, we have
    \begin{equation}
        R(\hat f_D) -\inf_{f \in \cH} R(f) \le {\left(2\frac{\log \frac{\vert\mathcal H\vert}{\delta} }{nh^\alpha}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} + \frac{8\log \frac{\vert\mathcal H\vert}{\delta}}{3n}
    \end{equation}
    with probability at least $1-\delta$.

    \begin{proof}
        First, we apply Bernstein's inequality~(\ref{ineq: boundvar}) plus a union bound over $\mathcal G$ to the random variables $g_f(f(X_i),Y_i)$. We obtain that with probability at least $1-\delta$, $\forall g\in \mathcal G$ it holds
        \begin{equation}
           R(f)-R(f_\mathrm{opt}) - \left(\hat R_D (f) - \hat R_D (f_\mathrm{opt})\right)
           \le
           \sqrt{\frac{2 {\left(\left(R(f)-R(f_\mathrm{opt})\right)/h\right)}^\alpha \log\frac{\vert \cH\vert}{\delta}}{n}} + \frac{4 \log \frac{\vert\cH\vert}{\delta}}{3n}
        \end{equation}
        where we assume that $f\mapsto g_f$ is bijective.\footnote{$\star$ this assumption is a great source of improvements, as instead of $\vert \cH\vert$ the bound presents more in general $\vert \mathcal G\vert$ which might be smaller than the hypotheses set.}
        Then, we apply this bound to $f = \hat f_D$. Using that $\hat R_D (\hat f_D) - \hat R_D (f_\mathrm{opt}) \le 0$, we obtain that with probability at least $1-\delta$,
        \begin{equation}
            R(\hat f_D)-R(f_\mathrm{opt}) \le 
           \sqrt{\frac{2 {\left(\left(R(\hat f_D)-R(f_\mathrm{opt})\right)/h\right)}^\alpha \log\frac{\vert \cH\vert}{\delta}}{n}} + \frac{4 \log \frac{\vert\cH\vert}{\delta}}{3n}.
        \end{equation}
        Now let $Z \coloneq R(\hat f_D)-R(f_\mathrm{opt})\ge 0$ and $l \coloneq \log \frac{\vert\cH\vert}{\delta}$, obtaining
        \begin{align}
            Z &\le \sqrt{\frac{2Z^\alpha l}{nh^\alpha}} + \frac{4l}{3n}
            = Z^{\alpha/2} A + B
        \end{align}
        where $A = \sqrt{\frac{2l}{nh^\alpha}}$ and $B = \frac{4l}{3n}$. We want to apply Young's inequality (Lemma~\ref{lemma: young} in the Appendix) to $x = Z^{\alpha/2}, y = A, p = \frac{2}{\alpha}, q = \frac{2}{2-\alpha}$. We obtain that
        \begin{equation}
            Z^{\alpha/2} A \le \frac{\alpha}{2} Z + \frac{2-\alpha}{2} A^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}
        \end{equation}
        since $x^p = Z$ and $y^q = A^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}}$. Plugging it in, 
        \begin{equation}
            Z \le \frac{\alpha}{2} Z + \frac{2-\alpha}{2} A^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}} + B
            \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
            \frac{2-\alpha}{2} Z \le \frac{2-\alpha}{2} A^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}} + B
        \end{equation}
        and dividing by $\frac{2-\alpha}{2}>0$ we get 
        \begin{equation}
            Z \le A^{\frac{2}{2-\alpha}} + \frac{2B}{2-\alpha}
            = {\left(\frac{2l}{nh^\alpha}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} + \frac{8l}{3n(2-\alpha)}.
        \end{equation}
        Finally, $\alpha\in(0,1]$ implies $2-\alpha\ge 1$, so the last term is at most $\frac{8l}{3n}$. Hence, with probability at least $1-\delta$, 
        \begin{equation}
            R(\hat f_D) - R(f_\mathrm{opt}) \le {\left(\frac{2\log\frac{\vert\cH\vert}{\delta}}{nh^\alpha}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2-\alpha}} + \frac{8\log\frac{\vert\cH\vert}{\delta}}{3n}.
        \end{equation}
        The second term is of order $\frac{l}{n}$, which is of lower order than the first one when $\alpha<1$ (as $\frac{1}{2-\alpha}<1$), while for $\alpha=1$ they are of the same order and the bound is $O\left(\frac{l}{n}\right)$: the fast rate.
    \end{proof}
\end{theorem}

This means that $h$ controls how distant the a posteriori probability is from $1/2$: if $h$ is small, then this probability may be close to $1/2$ which means we have a large noise in the data, if $h$ is large we have less noise. This whole reasoning is valid when $\alpha = 1$. More generally, the correct interpretation of these two values is given by the assumption on the variance.

\subsection{Bounds with growth function}
% This notes are written with the file from Loz which he sent on Oct 8 in Slack
We try here a different approach to obtaining the bounds above, using the growth function. Let us recall the first useful result. 

{\color{red} Notation fix}: Let us use $h$ to indicate functions in $\cH$ and $\ell_h:\X\times\Y \to \{0,1\}$ to indicate functions in $\LL \coloneq \ell \circ \cH$, with $\ell_h(x,y) = \ell(h(x),y)$.
\begin{theorem}[Empirical Rademacher bound]\label{thm:rad}
\cite[Theorem~3.3, equation~(3.4)]{MRT2018}
With probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rad-bound}
 R(\hat h)-\hat R(\hat h)\le 2\Rad(\LL)
 +3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

This is equivalent to Proposition~\ref{prop: symmetriz} plus a concentration result, just take the expectation over $D$. 

\begin{definition}[Growth function]
    The growth function of $\cH$ is
\[
 \Pi_{\cH}(n)\coloneq\sup_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X}
 \left|\left\{(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)):h\in\cH\right\}\right|.
\]
For an observed sample, define
\[
 N(D)=\left|\left\{(\ell_h(x_1,y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(x_n,y_n)):h\in\cH\right\}\right|.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Growth function]\label{lem:growth}
For every sample $D$ of size $n$,
\[
 1\le N(D)\le\Pi_{\cH}(n)\le\min\{|\cH|,2^n\}.
\]
% qui D è la realizzazione forse, otherwise come la confronti con \Pi ? 

\begin{proof}
    $N(D) \le \Pi_\cH(n)$ because the number of distinct loss vector can at most decrease, given prediction vectors. $\Pi_\cH(n)\le \card$ because the realizations of functions in $h$ are at most as much as the functions in $h$ (equality when functions in $h$ are bijective). $\Pi_\cH(n)\le 2^n$ because we have at most $2^n$ sequences of $n$ $1$s and $-1$s. 
\end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{corollary}[Growth-function bound]\label{cor:growth}
\cite[Section~3.2, Corollaries~3.8--3.9]{MRT2018}
For every sample,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: corollary1}
 \Rad(\LL)\le\sqrt{\frac{2\log N(D)}n}
 \le\sqrt{\frac{2\log\Pi_{\cH}(n)}n}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: corollary2}
 R(\hat h)-\hat R(\hat h)\le
 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\Pi_{\cH}(n)}n}
 +3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.
\end{equation}
The same bound holds with $\Pi_{\cH}(n)$ replaced by $N(D)$.

\begin{proof}
    Inequaility~(\ref{eq: corollary1}) is Massart's lemma applied to the set $\LL$. Inequaility~(\ref{eq: corollary2}) is Theorem 2.12 + (59). 
\end{proof}
\end{corollary}

Now let us apply the last inequality of Lemma 2.14 to the bound in (60).  
\begin{align*}
2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\Pi_{\cH}(n)}n} &\le \min \left\{ 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log\card}n},2\sqrt{\frac{2\log2^n}n} \right\}.
% &\le \min \left\{ 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log }n},2\sqrt{\frac{2n \log2}n} \right\}\\
% & \le \min \left\{ 2\sqrt{\frac{2 d \log2}n},2\sqrt{2\log2} \right\}\\
% &\le \min \left\{ O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right),O(1) \right\}
\end{align*}

Hence, with probability at least $1-\delta$, 
\begin{equation}
    R(\hat h)-\hat R(\hat h)\le \min \left\{ O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\vert\cH_d\vert}{n}}\right),O(1) \right\}+3\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Refined bound with Massart}
Let us consider an alternative road. Recall 
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) = \expv_{\xi,D} \left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(h(X_i), Y_i)\right]
\]
and
\[
\expv_D[(R-\hat R) (\hat h)] 
% \le \expv_D[\sup_{h \in \cH}(R-\hat R) (h)] 
\le 2 \mathcal R_n(\LL)
\]
which holds because the bound is true for the supremum of the gap over $h\in\cH$ (Proposition~\ref{prop: symmetriz}), hence it is true for $\hat h$ whatever this function is. 

Then, the application of Massart lemma (Lemma~\ref{lemma: massart's lemma}) can be refined by counting functions in $\LL$ instead of $\cH$. 
\begin{lemma}[Massart]
    We have
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le \expv_D\left[M\sqrt{\frac{2\log N(D)}{n}}\right]
\]
where  $N(D)=\left|\left\{(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)):h\in\cH\right\}\right|$.
\end{lemma}

Then, using Lemma 2.14 
\[
N(D) \le \min\{\card, 2^n\}
\] 
or simply because given a dataset, the realizations of it through $\ell_h$ are surely less than or equal than those through $h$, which are less than or equal $\card$. On the other hand, $N(D)\le 2^n$ because we have at most $2^n$ $n$-long sequences of $0s$ and $1s$. 
Substituting
\[
\expv_D[(R-\hat R) (\hat h_d)] \le 2M\sqrt{\frac{2\min\{\log \vert\cH_d\vert, \log 2^n\}}{n}} \le \min\left\{O\left(\sqrt\frac{\log\vert\cH_d\vert}{n}\right), O(1)\right\}.
\]
where $\hat h_d$ is the ERM among trees of depth at most $d$.

\subsection{Localized Rademacher complexity}

Until now, we have seen that we have a bound \[\min \left\{\mathcal R_n(\mathcal H_d), O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)\right\}\] that saturates, but is not sensitive to the noise, the ratio $n/N$, or the complexity of the data-generating function. Then, in the last two sections we saw bounds capturing the variance of the predictor or the noise, but which do not saturate, instead they grow linearly in the depth $d$. In this section, we look for a bound that saturates and is aware of the structure of the learning problem.

The motivating idea comes from the observation that the Rademacher complexity measures the expressiveness of the whole hypotheses set $\cH$, as the supremum runs over all the functions in this set. However, we know that while learning, we will focus on a subset of the functions in $\cH$ that are close to the true function $f^*$, and this happens after a few splits. Therefore, it makes sense to try to consider a $\it local\/$ measure of complexity.

\end{equation}
