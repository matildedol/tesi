\section*{Introduction}
{\bf Setting}


Given a sample space $\Omega$, we have $X:\Omega \to \mathcal X = \{\bar x_1,\ldots,\bar x_N\}$ and $Y: \Omega \to \mathcal Y = \{-1, 1\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq: y = f(x)}
    Y = \mathrm{sign}\left( h^* (X) + \varepsilon \right)
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. We want to predict $Y$ from $X$ by observing a sample of $n\ll N$ identical realizations given by $D = {(X_i,Y_i)}_{i=1}^n$. We indicate the realizations of the data with lower-case $x$ and $y$. The data is sampled with a probability measure $p_{\mathcal X}$ and the law of $D$ is the joint law $p(x,y) = p_{\mathcal X}(x) \cdot p(y \vert x)$, where $p(y\vert x)$ is a Gaussian centered at $h^*(x)$ with variance $\sigma^2$. We look for a predictor $h\in\mathcal H$, the hypotheses set. We choose a loss function $\ell: \mathcal X \to \mathbb R^+$
% and call $f = \ell \circ h$
and a learning algorithm to learn the best predictor. 

Recall that the expected risk of a predictor $h\in\mathcal H$ is
\[
    R(h) = \mathbb E_D(\ell(h(X), Y)) = \sum_{\bar x\in\mathcal X} \sum_{\bar y \in\{-1,1\}} \ell(h(\bar x), \bar y) p(\bar x, \bar y).
\]
Since we do not know the joint law in general, we use the empirical risk
\[
    \hat R_D (h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(x_i), y_i)
\]
and select the predictor that minimizes this quantity (ERM), 
\[
    \hat h_D = \argmin_{h\in \mathcal H} \hat R_D (h)
\]
which, importantly, is a random variable of the data $D$. 

Specifically, in this thesis 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\cdot$] $\ell$ is the counting loss $\ell(h(x), y) = \mathbf{1} (h(x)\neq y) = \frac{1 - h(x)y}{2}$,
    \item[$\cdot$] $\mathcal H_d$ is the set of binary decision trees of depth at most $d$.
\end{itemize}
\bigskip 

{\bf Research question}

In machine learning, there exists a large number of algorithms, notably neural networks trained with gradient descent, which achieve great performances at prediction tasks on unseen data. We want to understand the relationship between the behavior of such algorithms on the available data (training set) and their future behavior on unseen data (test set). How can we predict how this future behavior will look like? 

This is the big question in learning theory, which a number of results have tried to answer. Recent works claim that the less sophisticated, classical theory fails to describe qualitatively the performance of learning algorihtms on unseen data. This thesis seeks to understand whether this is true by testing theoretical results against experimental evidences. 

We start from the relatively simple setting of an arbitrarily big but finite hypotheses set and binary decision trees. 

Aligning with works mentioned above, we aim to describe the shape of the test error curve of decision trees as their depth increases.
To do so, we look for a function that bounds the gap between the expected and the empirical risk of the ERM, \[\expv_D\left[(R - \hat R_D)(\hat h_D)\right]\] We want this bound to capture the behavior of the curve qualitatively as well as changes in the structure of the learning problem (e.g.\ we look for sensitivity to the noise level $\sigma$ or to the complexity of the underlying true function $h^*$). 

All proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app: proofs}. 
\bigskip 

{\bf Results}

The first result that we inspect uses Hoeffding inequality for the random variable $(R - \hat R)(h)$ and a union bound over $\mathcal H$ to obtain a bound for $\hat h_D$, which depends on the stochasticity of the data $D$. 

\section{Union bound} \label{sec: unionbound}
\begin{theorem}[Union bound]\label{thm: unionbound}
\begin{equation}\label{eq: union bound}
    \expv_D[R(\hat h_D) - \hat R_D (\hat h_D)] \,\, \le \,\, O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log \vert \mathcal H \vert}{n}}\right)
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}


We plot the bound~(\ref{eq: union bound}) to compare it with the empirical behavior of the test error. 

{\bf Experimental design.} The support $\mathcal X$ is a discretization over $N$ numbers of the interval $[0,10]$ with a uniform measure. The law to generate the $y$s is~(\ref{eq: y = f(x)}). Our first choice of $h^*$ is a parabola centered horizontally at $5$, the midpoint of the support $\mathcal X$, and vertically so that roughly half of the points fall above the $x$-axis and half below.  Then, for the sake of flexibility, we pick a more complex function, a sinusoid. We maintain this choice throughout our experiments. Hence, we generate the $y$s with 
\[
    %  c = (np.pi * (num_crossings +1)) / u
    %  return 5*np.sin(c*x)
    h^*: x \mapsto 5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right)
\]
where $m$ is the number of times $h^*$ crosses the $x$-axis in the open interval $(0,10)$, yielding $m+1$ contiguous blocks of $1s$ and $-1s$. 
 Also, we test a Bernoullian noise instead of a Gaussian, concluding that there is no interesting difference in the performance of the algorithm as long as the portion of misclassified inputs due to the noise is the same. Hence, we stick to $\varepsilon = \sigma e$, with $e \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$. All in all, our law is
 \[
    y \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}\left(5\, \sin\left(\frac{\pi \,( m  +1)}{10}\, x\right) + \sigma e\right).
 \]
 Default values are $\sigma = 5$ and $m = 10$. Notice also that our $\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)$ maps to $1$ a value of $0$. 

We proceed as follows. We generate a test set with size $n_\text{test} = 200,000$. Then, for each depth $d \in [1, 7\lceil{\log_2 n}\rceil] \subset \mathbb N$ we do the following. For a number $T=100$ of iterations, we 
\begin{itemize}
    \item[$\cdot$] generate a train set ($n= 50$);
    \item[$\cdot$] fit a decision tree with depth at most $d$;
    \item[$\cdot$] compute the train error, the test error, and the gap between the two.
\end{itemize}
Then, we average the resulting quantities over the $T$ samples, obtaining empirical estimates of their expected values over the disorder of the data. 

Finally, we do the following plots:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the train vs.\ the test error as $d$ increases;
    \item the gap vs.\ the theoretical bound $\sqrt{\log \vert \mathcal H \vert/n}$;
    \item the test error vs.\ the train error + the bound.
\end{enumerate}

To plot the bound, we need to find the number of trees for a given maximum depth. The exact computation gives
\[
\vert \mathcal H_d \vert = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\min{N-1, 2^d-1}} \binom{N-1}{k}
\]

\section{Rademacher Complexity and Massart Lemma}\label{sec: rademmassart}

% The proof follows by bounding the supremum of the expected gap with the rademacher complexity via a symmetrization trick, and then applying Massart's lemma (Hoeffding lemma + $\expv \sup \le \sup \expv$ + bounding the sup with the sum). 


\begin{definition}[Rademacher complexity]
%Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ be Rademacher variables taking value $1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. The Rademacher complexity of the set $\LL = \{\ell \circ h : h \in \mathcal H \}$ is
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) = \expv_{\xi,D} \left[\sup_{\ell_h\in\LL}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i \ell(h(X_i), Y_i)\right]
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Rademacher complexity bound]\label{thm: rad bound}
%The gap between the expected and empirical risk of the ERM $\hat h$ is
\[
\expv_D[(R-\hat R) (\hat h)] 
% \le \expv_D[\sup_{h \in \cH}(R-\hat R) (h)] 
\le 2 \mathcal R_n(\LL)
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Massart]\label{lemma: refined massart}
%We have
\[
\mathcal R_n(\LL) \le \expv_D\left[\sqrt{\frac{2\log N_D(\cH)}{n}}\right]
\]
where  $N_D(\cH)=\left|\left\{(\ell_h(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,\ell_h(X_n,Y_n)):h\in\cH\right\}\right|$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Counting inequalities]\label{lemma: counting}
\[
N_D(\cH) \le \min\{\card, 2^n\}.
\] 
\end{lemma} 

Putting together this results, we obtain the final bound. 
\begin{theorem}[Rademacher and Massart bound]\label{thm: rademmassart bound}
\begin{align}
\expv_D[(R-\hat R) (\hat h_d)]&\le 2 \expv_D\left[\sqrt{\frac{2\log N_D(\cH)}{n}}\right] \notag \\
&\le 2\sqrt{\frac{2\min\{\log \vert\cH_d\vert, \log 2^n\}}{n}} \notag \\
&\le \min\left\{O\left(\sqrt\frac{\log\vert\cH_d\vert}{n}\right), O(1)\right\} \label{eq: rademmassart bound}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}

We plot the bound~(\ref{eq: rademmassart bound}), with as constant term $0.5$, in Figures~\ref{fig:pic2} and~\ref{fig:pic3}. The experimental design is the same as before.

\section{Dependence on the Best Risk}\label{sec: bestrisk}
We now try to obtain a bound depending on the noise. 

Set 
\[
R^*_\cH = \min_{h\in\cH} R(h).
\]
We aim at obtaining a bound on the gap at $\hat h$ in terms of the best risk $R^*_\cH$. This is because the best risk can be decomposed as 
\[
R^*_\cH = \underbrace{R^*}_{\text{Bayes risk}} + \underbrace{R^*_\cH - R^*}_{\text{Approximation error}} =: \sigma^2 + A_\cH
\]

\begin{theorem}[Bound with empirical risk]\label{thm: bound emprisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    R(\hat h) - \hat R(\hat h) \le \sqrt{\frac{\hat R(\hat h)\log(2N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}} + 16\frac{\log(2N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
    where $N_{n}(\cH) := \sup_{(x_1,y_1), \ldots,(x_{n},y_{n}) \in \X \times \Y} N_D(\cH)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Bound with best risk]\label{coroll: bound bestrisk}
    With probability at least $1-\delta$,
    \[
    R(\hat h) - \hat R(\hat h) \le \sqrt{\frac{R^*_{\cH}\log(4N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}
    \]
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Noise and approximation error bound]\label{coroll: bound noiseandappr}
With probability $1-\delta$,
\[
    R(\hat h) - \hat R(\hat h) \le \sqrt{\frac{(A_\cH + \sigma^2)\log(4N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}} + 20\frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}
\]
\end{corollary}

Where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\sigma^2$ measures the irreducible classification error due to noise;
    \item $A_\cH$ measures the approximation error of the hypotheses class.
\end{itemize}

After performing tail integration and applying Lemma 2.4 to a data size of $2n$, we obtain 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: noiseandappr bound}
\expv_D\left[R(\hat h)-\hat R(\hat h)\right] \le \min\left\{ O\left(
\sqrt{\frac{(A_{\mathcal H}+\sigma^2)\log \card}{n}} + \frac{\log\card}{n} \right), O\left(
\sqrt{A_{\mathcal H}+\sigma^2}+1  \right)\right\}
\end{equation}
and we plot it in Figure~\ref{fig:pic4} and Figure~\ref{fig:pic5}. 

{\color{red} Disclaimer:} For the second term in the min, we plot $\sqrt{A_{\mathcal H}+\sigma^2}$ directly without the $+1$, although I am not sure this is motivated, since $R^*_\cH <1$ as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pic7}. 

{\bf Experimental design:} We consider a Bernoullian noise $b\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ and generate
\[
y = \sgn(h^*(x))\cdot b
\]
flipping the labels of a portion $p$. This makes it easier to estimate the Bayes risk $R^*$, which is 
\[
R^* = 1-p
\]
for $p\ge 0.5$. We generally use $p=0.8$. $R^*_\cH$ is computed exactly using dynamic programming.

\section{Growth function}\label{sec: growthf}

\begin{definition}[Growth function]
    The growth function of $\cH$ is
\[
 \Pi_{n}(\mathcal H):=\sup_{x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\mathcal X}
 \left|\left\{(h(x_1),\ldots,h(x_n)):h\in\cH\right\}\right|.
\]
\end{definition}

The following extends Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}.

\begin{lemma}[Counting inequalities with growth function]\label{lem:growth}
For every sample $D$ of size $n$,
\[
 1\le N_D(\mathcal H)\le\Pi_{n}(\mathcal H)\le\min\{\vert \cH \vert,2^n\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\Pi_n(\mathcal H)  = N_n(\mathcal H) = \sup_{(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)\in \mathcal X \times \mathcal Y} N_D(\mathcal H)
\]
% qui D è la realizzazione forse, otherwise come la confronti con \Pi ?
\end{lemma}

The relation between $N_D$ and $\Pi_n$ makes it immediate to obtain growth-function versions of the bounds in Sections~\ref{sec: rademmassart} and~\ref{sec: bestrisk} (substitute $N(D)\le\Pi_n$ into Lemma~\ref{lemma: refined massart} and $N_{2n} = \Pi_{2n}$ into Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}). Substituting also $\Pi_{n}(\mathcal H)\le\min\{\vert \cH \vert,2^n\}$ yields bounds~(\ref{eq: rademmassart bound}) and (\ref{eq: noiseandappr bound}).

\section{Proofs}\label{app: proofs}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: unionbound}]
  Apply Hoeffding Lemma and a union bound over the hypotheses set $\mathcal H$. Tail integrate to obtain a result in expected value. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}]
  Symmetrization technique. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: refined massart}]
  Pass to the supremum over {\it loss vectors\/} instead of functions $\ell\circ h$, and bound it with the sum, which makes the $N(D)$ term appear. Then, apply the exponential Hoeffding Lemma and optimize for the coefficient in the exponent. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}]
  Given a dataset, its realizations through $\ell_h$ are less than or equal to those through $h$ because applying $\ell$ cannot increase the number of outputs, and the number of images of the functions in $\mathcal H$ are less than or equal to the number of functions $\card$. On the other hand, $N_D(\cH)\le 2^n$ because we have at most $2^n$ $n$-long sequences of $0s$ and $1s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: rademmassart bound}]
  Apply Theorem~\ref{thm: rad bound}, Lemma~\ref{lemma: refined massart} and Lemma~\ref{lemma: counting}. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}]
        Apply the relative deviation inequality (see Loz's file),
        \[
        \Pr\left[\exists h\in\cH : R(h)-\hat R(h) > \varepsilon \sqrt{R(h)}\right] \le 2N_{2n} (\cH) e^{-2\varepsilon^2/16}
        \]
        with the suitable $\varepsilon$ and then isolate $\hat R(\hat h)$ on the RHS.
        % I have not looked precisely at the proof of the rel dev ineq
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk}]
         Set $h_0 = \argmin_{h\in\cH} R(h)$, so that $R(h_0) = R^*_{\cH}$. Then,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: hatR h le hat R h0}
        \hat R(\hat h) \le \hat R(h_0).
        \end{equation}
        Since $h_0$ is independent of the data, $\expv_D[\hat R(h_0)] = R(h_0)$ and hence Bernstein gives, with probability at least $1-\delta/2$,
        \[
        \hat R(h_0) \le R^*_{\cH} + \sqrt{\frac{2R*_\cH \log (2/\delta)}{n}} + \frac{\log(2/\delta)}{3n}.
        \]
        Since $N_{2n}(\cH)\ge 1$ and thus $\frac{\log (2/\delta)}{n}\le \frac{\log(4N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}$,
        \begin{equation}\label{eq: bound hatR h0}
        \hat R(h_0) \le \left(\sqrt{R^*_\cH} + \sqrt{\frac{4\log(N_{2n}(\cH)/\delta)}{n}}\right)^2
        \end{equation}
        Substitute~(\ref{eq: hatR h le hat R h0}) into~(\ref{eq: bound hatR h0}), and apply the bound to $\hat R(\hat h)$ in the RHS of Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk} at confidence $1-\delta/2$. This yields the desired bound, which holds with probability at least $1-\delta$ by a union bound on the events ((\ref{eq: bound hatR h0}) and Theorem~\ref{thm: bound emprisk}) that we require to hold simultaneously.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound noiseandappr}]
  Substitute $R^*_\cH = \sigma^2 + A_\cH$ into Corollary~\ref{coroll: bound bestrisk}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:growth}]
    $N_D(\cH) \le \Pi_{n}(\cH)$ because the number of distinct loss vector can at most decrease, given prediction vectors. $\Pi_{n}(\cH)\le \card$ because the realizations of functions in $h$ are at most as much as the functions in $h$ (equality when functions in $h$ are bijective). $\Pi_{n}(\cH)\le 2^n$ because we have at most $2^n$ sequences of $n$ $1$s and $-1$s.
\end{proof}
